# Why local councils have not done more electronics reuse and resale

**Research date:** 2 October 2026 (AEST)

## Executive finding

The evidence does **not** support the simple explanation that local councils "do not care about reuse".

Dubbo Regional Council has planned/business-cased reuse shops. Narromine actively promotes reuse through Garage Sale Trail. NetWaste's regional strategy supports circular-economy/resource-recovery goals.

The deeper problem is structural:

> **Recycling is a collection-and-contract problem. Electronics reuse is an IT asset-disposition, retail, safety, data-security, repair and inventory problem.**

A council can often manage recycling by:
- accepting a separated stream;
- aggregating volume;
- contracting transport;
- recording tonnes;
- sending material to a processor.

Whole-device electronics reuse instead requires:
- triage;
- ownership/provenance;
- secure storage;
- data sanitisation;
- hardware testing;
- repair;
- electrical/product safety;
- pricing;
- retail/customer service;
- returns;
- unsold-stock management;
- battery/fire controls;
- final recycling.

That extra complexity explains much of the gap.

---

## 1. Dubbo has not ignored reuse

In October 2024 Dubbo Regional Council said staff were preparing a business case for **two reuse shops**:
- Whylandra;
- Wellington.

Council said the business case would assess:
- environmental benefits;
- economic benefits;
- social benefits.

Earlier Council material also said its facility master planning had identified tip/reuse shops as important to the local circular economy and landfill diversion.

As of 2 October 2026, this research did not find a current official page showing those shops operating.

### Interpretation

The concept appears to have stalled somewhere between:
**good idea → master plan → business case → funded/staffed operation**.

That is different from rejection of reuse.

---

## 2. NSW councils have documented exactly why reuse shops are hard

A 2026 Cessnock City Council report is one of the clearest current NSW examples.

Cessnock said industry practice has shifted away from councils operating reuse shops inside waste centres because of:
- **safety risk**;
- **compliance risk**;
- **security risk**;
- **financial risk**.

Its previous reuse shop closed after Council found risks/costs outweighed benefits.

The 2026 report said a council reuse shop would require:
- minimum **two staff at all times** for safety/security;
- reallocation of existing operational staff;
- security systems and after-hours patrols;
- electrical test-and-tag compliance;
- product-safety/liability management;
- point-of-sale systems and transaction costs;
- environmental-licence compliance;
- civil works;
- dedicated building;
- fencing/security infrastructure;
- utilities;
- monitoring;
- possible waste-levy exposure from illegal dumping.

It also said reuse shops attract after-hours dumping, weather exposure and pilfering.

Cessnock ultimately described partnerships with charities/op-shops as a lower-risk, more financially sustainable reuse model than a council-operated retail outlet.

Source:
- Cessnock City Council Supplementary Agenda, 18 February 2026, BN1/2026.

### Why this matters to Dubbo

This is not evidence that Dubbo's business case has reached the same conclusion.

It is strong NSW comparative evidence for the types of barriers Dubbo's own business case has to address.

## 3. Electronics are harder than ordinary reuse-shop goods

A reuse shop can sell:
- books;
- crockery;
- furniture;
- garden items;
- simple household goods.

Electronics add:
- personal/business data;
- passwords/accounts;
- activation locks;
- device-management locks;
- batteries;
- chargers;
- electrical safety;
- product recalls;
- obsolete operating systems;
- failure/return risk;
- customer expectations of functionality.

NSW Government guidance says second-hand electrical goods are covered by electrical-safety law and sellers/suppliers have obligations to ensure safety.

Businesses also have Australian Consumer Law/product-safety duties.

Sources:
- https://www.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-construction/safety-home/electrical-safety/buying-and-using-electrical-appliances
- https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/selling-goods-and-services/selling-safe-products

### Result

A laptop cannot responsibly be treated as:
**"looks okay → $50 sticker → shelf."**

Someone needs to establish what it is, whether it is safe, whether data has been removed, whether it works and what defects must be disclosed.

## 4. Data security is a major barrier

Cyber.gov.au warns that discarded/sold/gifted devices can expose:
- passphrases;
- credit-card details;
- personal messages;
- business information.

It recommends:
- backup;
- account/device unlinking;
- removable-media removal;
- data erase/factory reset;
- additional specialist help for sensitive data or difficult storage.

Source:
- https://www.cyber.gov.au/protect-yourself/securing-your-devices/how-secure-your-device/how-dispose-your-device-securely

For council or business fleets, the expected standard can be higher:
- serialised custody;
- documented sanitisation;
- verification;
- destruction where sanitisation fails.

This is why specialist ITAD operators such as PonyUp, Sircel, Compnow and WorkVentures sell **trust and auditability**, not merely recycling.

## 5. Lithium batteries create a waste-site and storage hazard

NSW's battery-fire problem has intensified.

The NSW EPA reported:
- **332 lithium-ion battery-related incidents in 2025**;
- by 7 April 2026, **62 battery fires in the community**;
- at least **12** in garbage trucks/waste facilities/tips;
- a further **103 waste-industry fires suspected** to involve batteries.

Source:
- https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/news/epamedia/260407-nsw-leads-the-country-in-battery-reform-to-fight-fires-and-pollution

A reuse operation holding unknown:
- laptops;
- tablets;
- phones;
- powerbanks;
- chargers

therefore needs real battery triage and isolation, especially for:
- swelling;
- impact damage;
- water damage;
- heat/fire exposure;
- unknown charging history.

That is a different risk profile from selling books/furniture.

## 6. Labour is both the benefit and the cost

NSW EPA reuse research found that in 2022–23 reuse:
- generated **25 times more jobs per tonne than recycling**;
- generated **81 times more jobs per tonne than landfill**;
- diverted about **49,900 tonnes** to reuse;
- delivered large community/economic benefits.

Source:
- https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/Working-together/Partnerships-with-the-EPA/Measuring-impacts-of-reuse

This is good policy news, but it also reveals why reuse costs more operationally.

Every tonne of reuse requires far more:
- hands-on sorting;
- inspection;
- cleaning;
- repair;
- presentation;
- sales/customer work.

That labour is valuable, but it has to be funded.

## 7. Regional NSW has a scale and distance problem

NetWaste's Regional Waste and Sustainable Materials Strategy 2023–27 explicitly identifies regional disadvantages.

Moving east to west:
- local processing options become more limited;
- distances to processors/end markets increase;
- transport becomes more expensive;
- western councils have smaller populations and rate bases;
- waste budgets are lower;
- service levels vary;
- economies of scale are reduced;
- staff/skills/service-provider capacity is thinner.

Source:
- NetWaste Regional Waste and Sustainable Materials Strategy 2023–27.

This is especially relevant to:
- Narromine;
- Trangie;
- smaller western towns.

### Why a 6,000-person LGA behaves differently from Sydney

A reuse operation needs enough:
- good incoming stock;
- buyers;
- technical labour;
- retail traffic;
- margin

to cover fixed costs.

Small regional councils can encourage reuse much more cheaply through:
- Garage Sale Trail;
- marketplaces;
- charities;
- repair events;
- partnerships.

Narromine's 2024 Garage Sale Trail result — **4,231 items reused/sold across 47 sales/stalls** — is an example of reuse without council-owned inventory.

## 8. Bad donations and dumping can destroy reuse economics

NSW EPA says damaged and illegally dumped donations cost charities **millions of dollars each year** in cleanup/disposal.

Source:
- https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/Your-environment/Illegal-dumping/responsible-donating

The problem is particularly dangerous for electronics because an unattended "donation" can be:
- smashed TV;
- stripped PC;
- swollen battery;
- water-damaged laptop;
- obsolete printer;
- cracked monitor.

If a reuse site becomes an unofficial free dump, the operator inherits:
- handling cost;
- storage cost;
- disposal cost;
- fire risk.

This is why the proposed Dubbo eWaste Phase 0 model insists on:
- pre-approval;
- no unattended drop-off;
- no public waste bin;
- strict refusal rights.

---

## 9. Recycling infrastructure is institutionally easier than reuse

Councils already have:
- waste facilities;
- weighbridges;
- collection contracts;
- regional procurement;
- NTCRS/product-stewardship programs;
- recycler relationships;
- tonnage reporting.

The system naturally supports:

**collection point → contracted transporter → recycler → material recovery**

TechCollect makes the distinction especially clear:
- its normal collection stream is for recycling;
- collected equipment is not sold second-hand for reuse;
- owners are encouraged to pass working equipment on **before** using TechCollect.

See:
- `TECHCOLLECT-REUSE-DEEP-DIVE.md`

### Policy incentive problem

NTCRS measures:
- collection;
- recycling;
- material recovery.

It does not impose an equivalent whole-device reuse target requiring:
- power-on testing;
- repair;
- resale;
- redeployment.

So the easiest auditable council outcome is often:
**tonnes safely recycled**, not **devices saved whole**.

## 10. Licensing, provenance and stolen-goods risk

NSW second-hand dealer law adds another layer when prescribed second-hand electronics are acquired/resold commercially.

Current project research has already established:
- electronic goods are prescribed;
- ID/ownership records can apply;
- reporting requirements can apply;
- a 14-day hold/no-alteration rule can apply;
- the "recycling and rubbish collection programs" exemption remains unresolved for the proposed mixed reuse/resale model.

See:
- `LEGAL-LICENSING.md`
- `BACKLOG-01-05-PLANNING-LEGAL.md`

A council or operator therefore needs a clear legal model for:
- ownership transfer;
- suspected stolen property;
- resale;
- records;
- storage.

---

## 11. Consumer and warranty risk

A recycler can destroy a failed laptop.

A reseller has to deal with the buyer.

Second-hand electronics sold by a business can bring:
- Australian Consumer Law obligations;
- acceptable-quality expectations;
- known-defect disclosure;
- refund/remedy handling;
- product-safety/recall duties.

That is another reason councils often prefer to:
- facilitate reuse;
- partner with charities;
- tender to social enterprises;
rather than become electronics retailers themselves.

---

## 12. Storage and inventory depreciation

Electronics lose value quickly.

A reused chair may retain roughly the same value sitting for months.

A business laptop can lose value because of:
- age;
- operating-system support;
- battery degradation;
- new hardware releases;
- market price falls.

PonyUp and The Laptop Initiative both illustrate the importance of moving equipment quickly:
- PonyUp targets recent corporate refreshes;
- The Laptop Initiative deliberately tries to avoid warehousing and matches incoming laptops to demand.

This is another reason a council tip shop without specialist sales channels can struggle.

---

## 13. Why Narromine's approach makes sense

Narromine's Garage Sale Trail model pushes the hard work back to:
- the owner;
- the buyer.

Council promotes the event, but generally does not need to:
- take custody;
- test;
- wipe;
- store;
- price;
- warranty;
- dispose of unsold items.

That is a rational low-cost reuse strategy for a small LGA.

It does not solve secure reuse of:
- corporate fleets;
- government devices;
- data-bearing electronics.

That remains a specialist gap.

---

## 14. Why Dubbo is different

Dubbo has:
- greater population;
- greater waste volume;
- stronger regional-hub role;
- more businesses/institutions;
- more potential buyers;
- existing digital-inclusion organisations;
- enough scale to justify examining reuse shops.

That is why Dubbo Regional Council has progressed further than many nearby councils toward a formal reuse-shop concept.

But an **electronics reuse operation** is still more specialised than a general tip shop.

---

## 15. The structural gap

The evidence suggests the missing actor is not another council waste facility.

It is a small specialist **regional IT asset recovery / refurbishment layer** that sits before disposal.

Potential flow:

**business / household / institution**
→ pre-screened reuse operator
→ ownership record
→ secure sanitisation
→ test / grade
→ refurbish
→ resale or social redeployment
→ donor parts
→ verified recycling for failures

Council can continue doing what it does well:
- mass collection;
- statutory waste management;
- downstream procurement;
- material recycling.

The specialist can do what waste facilities are not designed to do:
- preserve whole-device value.

---

## 16. Implications for Dubbo eWaste

The barriers justify the existing Phase 0 rules:

- small scale;
- pre-approved intake;
- no unattended dumping;
- no public battery collection;
- high-quality/recent devices first;
- secure data workflow;
- known destination before acceptance;
- quick inventory turnover;
- online resale;
- verified downstream recycler;
- measure labour and days-to-sale;
- scale only if reuse economics are proven.

See:
- `PHASE-0-OPERATING-BLUEPRINT.md`
- `INTAKE-POLICY.md`

## 17. Best council-partnership proposition

Instead of asking Council:

> "Can I run your tip shop?"

a lower-risk proposition is:

> **"Can we pilot pre-disposal diversion of selected reusable electronics, with documented ownership, secure data handling and a guaranteed recycling outlet for failures?"**

That avoids many of the reasons councils struggle with reuse shops:
- no open dump;
- no uncontrolled public retail at the waste facility;
- no need for Council staff to diagnose laptops;
- no need for Council to provide IT warranty/support;
- measurable diversion outcome.

Any such model still needs council/legal/contract approval.

## Final conclusion

The towns have not failed to notice reuse.

They are operating inside a system where:

**recycling is easier to contract, measure and regulate than whole-device reuse.**

The strongest opportunity for Dubbo eWaste is therefore to become the missing specialist layer **before** the existing waste system, not to recreate the whole waste system.
