# Dubbo Circular Economy in Practice

**Deep research date:** 8 October 2026  
**Scope:** Dubbo, NSW, with Wellington and nearby regional comparisons where they clarify Dubbo's options.  
**Core question:** When something in Dubbo becomes broken, unwanted, surplus or obsolete, what practical opportunities exist to keep it in use before it is recycled or landfilled?

## Evidence labels

- **VERIFIED CURRENT** — current primary/authoritative source supports the claim.
- **VERIFIED HISTORICAL** — primary/strong evidence supports an earlier state, not assumed current.
- **STRONG LEAD** — credible evidence identifies a likely pathway but direct operational confirmation is still required.
- **LIKELY** — reasonable inference from multiple facts, clearly separated from direct evidence.
- **UNVERIFIED** — specific claim could not be established.
- **PROPOSED / FUTURE** — planned, strategic or under investigation; not an operating service.

# Executive answer — how circular is Dubbo really?

Dubbo has a **real circular economy, but it is fragmented and uneven**.

The strongest circular activity is not necessarily labelled "circular economy". It is ordinary economic behaviour in sectors where the item still has enough value to justify labour, parts and logistics:

- vehicle servicing, dismantling and recycled/reconditioned parts;
- farm-machinery repair, field servicing and parts supply;
- power-tool, mower and outdoor-equipment repair;
- bicycle repair;
- clothing alterations and repair;
- furniture restoration;
- computer and phone repair;
- op-shop resale and social redistribution.

Dubbo also has increasingly sophisticated **resource recovery**: FOGO compost returned to residents, local polystyrene densification, separated metals/whitegoods/e-waste, textile recycling, embedded-battery pathways, and an emerging solar-panel circular-economy agenda.

But those two systems are not well connected.

> **The strongest evidence-based description is: Dubbo has a conventional waste-and-recycling system surrounded by important pockets of repair, resale and reuse. It does not yet have a mature, universally accessible "repair/reuse before waste" system.**

Council itself recognises the distinction. Its Waste Strategy defines circular economy around keeping products and materials in use through reuse, repurposing, remanufacture and recycling. Council manages about **101,000 tonnes of waste per year**, of which about **28% is household-origin and 72% non-household**. Council reports about **45% diversion from landfill**, but its own strategy notes the true resource-recovery rate is lower because a large part of "diversion" is material retained onsite for landfill construction/capping/cover. Council says sorting **more than 35,000 tonnes per year of self-haul mixed waste** is one of its greatest opportunities.

The biggest practical circular-economy gap is therefore **not a lack of recycling**.

It is the point immediately before waste:

> **Who checks whether an item can remain a product before it becomes a waste stream?**

For most consumer goods, the answer is currently: **the owner must deliberately seek repair, resale or donation before disposal.** Once an item enters Whylandra, a bulky-rubbish collection or a retail e-waste recycling stream, the public evidence shows segregation for recycling and safe processing, but not a systematic product-level repairability assessment.

# 2. Electronics: laptops, desktops, monitors, phones, tablets, TVs, printers, routers and consoles

## Repair
**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Dubbo has multiple commercial computer/phone repairers, with current advertised services covering laptop/desktop diagnosis, upgrades, software repair, data recovery and custom PC work.

## Learn-to-repair
**GAP:** no formal inclusive Dubbo Repair Café, makerspace, hackspace or public computer-repair workshop was identified. Macquarie Regional Library Tech Help is digital help, not hardware repair.

## Refurbishment / resale
Local refurbished-computer supply exists. Direct resale, trade-in and private handover also keep electronics in use.

## Donation
A universal walk-in "donate your working/repairable computer, we sanitise/refurbish/redistribute it" pathway was not verified.

Vinnies NSW explicitly lists technology equipment such as computers, printers, scanners and laptops among items it does not accept as ordinary donations.

## Retail trade-in / stewardship
Officeworks has an important distinction:
- eligible trade-in devices can retain product value and move to another owner;
- ordinary Bring It Back e-waste goes to recycling.

JB Hi-Fi and normal TechCollect streams are primarily **recycling**, not second-hand reuse.

## Once it becomes e-waste
Whylandra accepts household e-waste and the NSW embedded-battery trial accepts whole relevant devices. Current public evidence does not show computer-specific repair/refurbishment triage once surrendered.

**Repair-before-recycling score: C — owners can seek repair/reuse, but the disposal system does not publicly demonstrate product-level repairability triage.**

### Further investigation
- Mystery-shop every repairer for repair-vs-replace criteria and customer-supplied-parts policy.
- Call Officeworks to confirm the exact 2026 Dubbo trade-in eligibility and downstream partner.
- Ask Council whether any e-waste at Whylandra is visually screened for reusable equipment.
- Identify the current post-June-2025 Council e-waste contractor and reuse clauses.
- Obtain annual Dubbo e-waste tonnage and device counts.
- Survey local businesses/schools for actual retired-device outcomes.
- Quantify the reusable fraction in a small controlled e-waste sample rather than assuming it.

# 3. Household appliances

Dubbo has real repair infrastructure, although it is less transparent online than computer repair.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Doug Smith Spares operates a Dubbo appliance-parts branch and supplies parts for most appliance brands. It does not perform repairs itself but says it can refer customers to repair trades. That is important circular infrastructure because spare-parts availability is a prerequisite for repair.

Local business listings and repair providers also show appliance/stove repair availability.

Whylandra accepts whitegoods for recycling; refrigerant-containing units have degassing requirements/fees. Bulky collection accepts whitegoods and small appliances/electronics.

The missing piece is a public, measurable bridge between those two systems. No evidence was found that a washing machine or fridge entering Council's bulky/waste stream is first assessed by an appliance repairer.

**Repair-before-recycling score: C.**

### Further investigation
- Identify every currently active appliance technician and their accepted brands.
- Obtain typical call-out/diagnostic charges and age thresholds for economic repair.
- Ask repairers which parts are most difficult to source and what percentage are "repairable but uneconomic".
- Ask Council whether reusable whitegoods are ever diverted from bulky collection.
- Ask Orana Support Service whether donated whitegoods are tested, repaired, referred out, rejected or redistributed as received.
- Trace rejected appliances to their exact downstream metal/e-waste processor.

# 4. Furniture and household goods

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Restoore Joinery & Furniture advertises furniture restoration, repair and upcycling in Dubbo, explicitly describing keeping quality timber furniture out of landfill.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Orana Support Service accepts clothing, household goods and selected furniture/whitegoods. It runs Dubbo and Wellington op shops and specifically seeks furniture and whitegoods for clients moving into long-term housing.

**Repair-before-recycling score: B/C — substantial reuse exists before disposal, but damaged/repairable goods can still fall through.**

- Determine whether Dubbo Men's Sheds repair donated furniture for resale.
- Trace mattresses separately: refurbishment restrictions, recycling contractor and destination.
- Quantify bulky-collection furniture and estimate reusable condition through a pilot audit.

# 5. Tools, mowers and equipment

This is one of Dubbo's strongest repair cultures.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Tool Repair Centre Dubbo (located in Geurie) explicitly states that tools are an investment and it is often cheaper to repair than replace. It repairs major brands including DeWalt, Paslode, Metabo, Makita and HiKOKI.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** M.M & Mechanical in Dubbo provides repairs, servicing and parts for mowers, quads, side-by-sides and other outdoor equipment and explicitly frames servicing as life extension.

This is a functioning circular economy because:
- parts are available;
- the equipment retains value;
- specialist labour exists;
- the owner has an economic reason to repair.

If a small battery tool is instead handed into an e-waste/battery stream, however, material recovery replaces repair.

**Repair-before-recycling score: B/C at system level; A/B where owners enter the repair market first.**

### Further investigation
- Ask repairers for annual repair volumes and "beyond economic repair" rates.
- Investigate whether failed tools are retained for donor parts.
- Map battery-tool battery rebuilding versus replacement.
- Confirm whether trade-ins or manufacturer repair programs operate locally.
- Measure what types of tools enter Whylandra/bulky waste.

# 6. Bicycles

**Repair-before-recycling score: C.**

# 7. Clothing and textiles

Clothing has several circular layers.

**Repair:** Macquarie Dry Cleaners has operated since 1956 and currently advertises clothing repair, alteration and restoration specifically to extend garment life.

**Reuse:** Dubbo op shops and social enterprises resell donated clothing.

**Fallback recycling:** Council offers residents and opportunity shops free textile recycling at Whylandra. Council says items that cannot be sold in store can be bagged and sent for recycling.

This is closer to a proper hierarchy:
**repair → reuse/resale → textile recycling → residual disposal**

However, the final textile downstream processor and the proportion genuinely recycled versus exported or rejected was not established.

**Repair-before-recycling score: B.**

### Further investigation
- Identify Council's textile-recycling contractor and final processing destination.
- Ask major op shops what proportion of donations sell locally, transfer, recycle and landfill.
- Determine whether unsold textiles are exported.
- Map seamstresses, upholstery and shoe-repair services.
- Quantify textile tonnage collected at Whylandra.

# 8. Construction and demolition materials

This is potentially one of the **largest tonnage opportunities**.

Council's Waste Strategy records about **38,525 tonnes of C&D waste for sorting/disposal** in the baseline breakdown, much larger than many household streams.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Boland Contracting says it reuses, recycles and salvages materials during demolition, including timber, roofing, tiles, brick, porcelain and concrete, and offers crushing that can return aggregate to a project.

This proves private-sector circular activity exists.

But Dubbo does not have a clearly identified public building-material reuse warehouse/exchange comparable to a mature salvage centre. Whylandra accepts/recycles material categories, but that is not the same as preserving doors, cabinetry, timber or fixtures as products.

**Repair/reuse-before-recycling score: C.**

### Further investigation
- Quantify direct-product salvage versus crushed/material recycling.
- Map all demolition contractors and their reuse percentages.
- Identify buyers of salvaged timber, doors, windows, kitchens and fixtures.
- Investigate a building-material exchange or salvage warehouse.
- Ask Council whether development/demolition approvals can require pre-demolition salvage plans.
- Obtain 2025/26 C&D tonnage by material and destination.
- Investigate pallet reuse and commercial timber flows.

# 9. Automotive circular economy

Automotive is arguably Dubbo's strongest mature circular-product system.

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Scott's 4WD Parts Plus describes more than 75 years in automotive parts recycling, vehicle dismantling, and more than a million new/recycled/reconditioned/refurbished parts. It says parts are inspected/tested and returned to use, while hazardous fluids/materials are managed separately.

Other wreckers/dismantlers operate locally, and ordinary mechanical repair is extensive.

The hierarchy is mature:
**maintain → repair → replace component → use recycled/reconditioned component → dismantle donor vehicle → recover parts → recycle remaining materials**

That is much closer to a genuine circular economy than most consumer e-waste.

**Repair-before-recycling score: A.**

### Lessons for electronics
- preserve the product/part before material shredding;
- maintain searchable inventory;
- grade/test used parts;
- provide warranties;
- make second-hand parts normal rather than exceptional;
- separate hazardous residuals from valuable reusable components.

### Further investigation
- Obtain actual annual vehicles dismantled/parts reused.
- Map alternator/starter/engine/transmission remanufacturing.
- Trace tyres, oils, lead-acid batteries and unreusable vehicle shells.
- Quantify jobs and revenue tied to automotive reuse.
- Test which automotive quality/warranty practices could transfer to electronics.

# 10. Agricultural machinery

**VERIFIED CURRENT:** Dubbo Machinery Service says it has serviced regional machinery for more than 40 years, stocks genuine/aftermarket parts and repairs all makes, with technicians receiving current industry training.

Regional agricultural businesses also provide mobile field service, hydraulics, spare parts, used machinery and major overhauls.

High asset values make repair economically rational in a way that a cheap kettle or low-end printer often is not.

**Repair-before-recycling score: A/B.**

- Investigate pumps, irrigation, GPS/precision-ag electronics and hydraulic-cylinder rebuilding separately.

# 13. Whylandra: what happens before recycling?

Whylandra is strong at **segregation and material recovery**.

Current Council material identifies separated streams including e-waste, metals, whitegoods, tyres, textiles, batteries, paint, polystyrene and other problem wastes. Council also prohibits unauthorised scavenging.

The current public Whylandra page does **not** advertise:
- a tip shop/reuse shop;
- a repair workshop;
- product-level reuse assessment;
- public salvage;
- an authorised pre-recycling refurbishment partner.

Council's pre-booked bulky collection is explicitly designed to improve sorting of recyclable and non-recyclable material. That is beneficial, but it is **recycling triage**, not proof of **repair/reuse triage**.

The clearest current conclusion is:

> **Once an item has crossed into the formal waste stream, Dubbo has relatively good ways to separate materials, but no publicly demonstrated system for asking "can this still be a product?" first.**

### Further investigation
Ask Council:
- Are Whylandra staff instructed to identify reusable items?
- Is there a designated reuse holding area not visible publicly?
- Who owns an item after deposit?
- Do current contracts allow Council to divert items before recycler collection?
- What liability/product-safety issues block reuse?
- How many usable goods are observed in self-haul/bulky streams?
- What happened to the 2024 reuse-shop business case?
- What happened after the draft Whylandra Master Plan was expected at the March 2026 meeting?
- Is a tip shop/reuse shop funded, designed or scheduled?
- Could a tightly controlled third party run a pilot reuse assessment?

# 14. Downstream chains

A key finding across multiple waste streams is that "recycled" is often where public transparency stops.

## Better-understood streams
- FOGO → compost, including resident compost giveaways;
- polystyrene → compacted blocks → manufacturers/products;
- automotive parts → direct reuse/reconditioned use;
- construction concrete → crushing/aggregate in some private projects.

## Partly understood
- e-waste → specialist material recovery; exact current Dubbo Council/AMR chain remains incomplete;
- textiles → bagged and sent for recycling; final processor/end markets not publicly established here;
- tyres → stewardship/material-recovery pathways, local final product chain not always item-level transparent;
- whitegoods/metals → metals recovery, exact downstream varies.

### Further investigation
For every major stream request:
- current contractor legal entity;
- first downstream facility;
- mass balance / recovery percentage;
- reuse versus material recycling;
- interstate/overseas destination;
- certificates/reports;
- residual landfill fraction.

# 19. Repair economics

Why repair happens:
- item retains meaningful value;
- parts are accessible;
- technician skills exist;
- repair is faster/cheaper than replacement;
- downtime matters;
- used parts have trusted markets.

Why repair fails:
- labour exceeds replacement value;
- parts are unavailable/proprietary;
- sealed/glued design;
- software/account locks;
- safety/liability;
- data/privacy;
- freight;
- cheap new products;
- owner does not know repair exists.

This explains why a tractor, transmission or professional power tool is often repaired while a cheap kettle may be discarded.

### Further investigation
Collect actual local numbers:
- average diagnosis fee;
- median labour time;
- part lead times;
- repair acceptance rate;
- repair success rate;
- "uneconomic repair" rate by category;
- avoided replacement value.

# 25. Right to repair

Australia has clearer repair-information infrastructure for motor vehicles than for many other products.

The Productivity Commission previously identified unnecessary barriers to repair. In 2026, Treasury is consulting on expanding right-to-repair arrangements to agricultural machinery and improving the Motor Vehicle Information Scheme.

This matters in Dubbo because agriculture/automotive are two of the strongest repair sectors.

### Further investigation
- Track whether agricultural right-to-repair reforms become law.
- Interview dealers/independent repairers on diagnostic software and parts restrictions.
- Investigate appliance/electronics parts availability under consumer-guarantee rules.
- Record manufacturer repair documentation barriers reported locally.

# 27. Biggest leakage points

## 1. Household / bulky goods → recycling or landfill
**Evidence: HIGH.** Bulky collection takes furniture, whitegoods, electronics, bikes and other potentially repairable goods. Sorting is designed around recyclable/non-recyclable streams, not publicly documented repairability.

## 2. Self-haul mixed waste → landfill
**Evidence: VERY HIGH / volume significant.** Council says >35,000 tonnes per year is a major opportunity.

## 3. Charity rejection → recycling or landfill
**Evidence: HIGH.** Charities must reject unsafe/damaged/unsellable goods. Textile recycling offers a good fallback for textiles, but other categories are less coherent.

## 4. Business equipment refresh → opaque external route
**Evidence: MEDIUM.** Formal systems exist, but local reuse outcomes are often not publicly reported.

## 5. Cheap appliance/electronic failure → replacement
**Evidence: MEDIUM.** Repair capability exists, but economics can favour replacement.

## 6. Construction → material recycling rather than component reuse
**Evidence: HIGH for C&D volume; MEDIUM for reusable-product loss.** Large tonnage exists and private salvage occurs, but no broad reusable-building-material marketplace was found.

## 7. Once deposited at Whylandra → no public salvage
**Evidence: HIGH.** Scavenging is prohibited; no operating public reuse shop is advertised.

### Further investigation
The single most valuable new dataset would be a **condition audit before disposal**:
- sample 200 bulky/self-haul items;
- classify working / simple repair / substantial repair / parts / material recycling / landfill;
- record category and estimated weight;
- do not remove items without formal Council approval.

# 33. Follow-the-item case studies

## Case 1 — five-year-old laptop, failed battery
Best path: local diagnosis → battery replacement → continued use.  
Alternative: trade-in/direct resale if otherwise functional.  
If surrendered as e-waste: material recycling pathway.  
**Leakage:** no universal community repair/refurb donation triage.

## Case 2 — ten-year-old washing machine, drain fault
Best path: appliance technician/parts → repair if economic.  
If uneconomic: whitegoods recycling/metal recovery.  
**Leakage:** no publicly documented repair assessment once in Council waste stream.

## Case 3 — working couch no longer wanted
Best path: family/Marketplace/charity/social-enterprise donation.  
If rejected or bulky-collected: recycling/disposal pathway.  
**Leakage:** damaged but repairable furniture can fall between charity acceptance and waste.

## Case 4 — bicycle needs tyres and brakes
Best path: bike-shop repair.  
Possible informal resale/donation.  
If owner declares it waste: bulky/waste route.  
**Leakage:** no verified bike refurbishment/redistribution program.

## Case 5 — broken cordless drill
Best path: specialist tool repair if quality/value justify it.  
If cheap/failed battery/electronics and owner skips repair: battery/e-waste pathway.  
**Leakage:** owner must know repair exists.

## Case 6 — office replaces 25 computers
Best path: serialised sanitisation → test/grade → resale/redeployment/donation → recycle failures.  
National ITAD providers can do this, but a simple small-batch Dubbo route remains fragmented.  
**Leakage:** data/security/logistics can push value-positive devices to bulk recycling.

## Case 7 — renovation removes doors, timber and cabinets
Best path: planned deconstruction → direct reuse/resale/salvage → crush/recycle residuals.  
**Leakage:** demolition sequencing can destroy reusable products before recovery.

## Case 8 — farm replaces machinery
Best path: repair/overhaul → used sale/trade → parts harvesting → metals recycling.  
This is one of the strongest circular pathways because the equipment retains high value.

### Further investigation
Turn these into real case studies by following one actual item through each pathway and recording:
- time;
- cost;
- kilometres;
- decision points;
- final owner/material destination;
- value retained.

# 34. Where a Repair Café fits

A Repair Café would fill a **real but specific** gap.

It should not attempt to replace:
- commercial computer repairers;
- appliance technicians;
- mechanics;
- machinery dealers;
- bike shops;
- furniture restorers.

Its strongest roles are:
- repairability triage;
- teaching owners;
- simple low-risk repairs;
- connecting people with specialist local repairers;
- helping people find parts;
- scam/digital guidance where relevant;
- collecting evidence on why items would otherwise be discarded;
- redirecting only true end-of-life items to recyclers.

## Good first categories
- computers/laptops;
- simple non-mains electronics;
- bikes;
- textiles;
- furniture/small mechanical items;
- simple tool maintenance where competent.

## Exclude initially
- exposed 240V electrical work without appropriately competent/licensed arrangements;
- gas appliances;
- swollen/damaged lithium batteries;
- safety-critical devices;
- complex medical equipment.

### Further investigation
- Council/venue insurance;
- public liability;
- volunteer competency;
- electrical safety boundaries;
- Repair Café International house rules;
- commercial referral protocol;
- battery exclusion;
- data/privacy consent for computers;
- 3-month demand measurement.

# 38. Final outputs

## A. How circular is Dubbo really?
**Moderately circular, but uneven.** Product-life extension is strong where markets already reward repair. Council resource recovery is substantial and improving. The missing layer is systematic, inclusive **reuse/repair before surrender to waste**.

## B. Ten strongest current circular activities
1. automotive recycled/reconditioned parts;
2. agricultural machinery repair;
3. tool/mower repair;
4. clothing repair/alteration;
5. bicycle repair;
6. furniture restoration;
7. charity/social-enterprise resale and redistribution;
8. computer/electronics repair;
9. FOGO compost returned to community;
10. separated material recovery including polystyrene, textiles, metals, e-waste and whitegoods.

## C. What is mostly recycling rather than reuse?
- normal e-waste take-back;
- embedded-battery collection;
- many retail stewardship schemes;
- whitegoods/scrap streams after disposal;
- tyres;
- polystyrene;
- unsellable textile recycling.

These are valuable but occur after the product has generally stopped being a product.

## D. What gets repaired before recycling?
Repair is common where the owner deliberately enters a repair market. It is not publicly demonstrated as a universal step inside the waste system.

## E. Ten biggest gaps
See Section 28. The top three are:
1. reuse intercept before waste;
2. inclusive community repair/learning;
3. building-material product salvage.

## F. Most important leakage
The owner-to-waste transition: once ownership/value is surrendered, the system optimises safe handling and material recovery rather than repair.

## G. Five low-cost Council actions
1. Support one tightly scoped Repair Café pilot and measure outcomes.
2. Run a supervised **condition audit** of bulky/self-haul goods before disposal; no removal needed.
3. Publish a live "Repair / Donate / Reuse / Recycle" directory by item.
4. Put repair/reuse prompts at bulky-booking and Whylandra decision points before disposal.
5. Release/collect better data separating product reuse, material recovery, onsite landfill use and landfill.

## H. Five community actions
1. Trial inclusive repair sessions.
2. Create a volunteer skill register.
3. Build referral links with local repair businesses.
4. Create "useful parts/materials" exchange days.
5. Partner with op shops to rescue low-risk repairable donations before rejection.

## I. Five business opportunities
1. electronics refurbishment/small-batch ITAD;
2. reusable building-material brokerage;
3. appliance repair/refurb + used parts;
4. tool/bike/furniture repair classes;
5. logistics/marketplace service for bulky reusable goods.

## J. Would a Repair Café matter?
**Yes, but it is not the biggest tonnage intervention.** It fills a real inclusion/skills/triage gap and can generate evidence. The biggest tonnage opportunities are likely mixed self-haul waste and C&D. A Repair Café is best understood as **community circular infrastructure and behaviour change**, not a replacement for major waste-processing investment.

## K. Lowest-cost pilot
Run one **3-hour, pre-booked repair-and-triage event**:
- 8–12 items;
- owners stay with items;
- simple laptop/PC, bike, textile/furniture/simple-mechanical categories;
- no damaged batteries;
- no unqualified mains electrical work;
- no overnight custody;
- commercial referral list;
- weigh/record each item;
- ask: "What would you have done if this event did not exist?"
- outcome: fixed / diagnosed / part needed / commercial referral / reuse / recycle.

Repeat monthly for three months only if demand exists.

## L. One recommended next action
At the Council meeting, ask for **one concrete thing**:

> **Would Council support a three-month Repair Café / repair-triage pilot and help define the measurements it would need to decide whether repair/reuse deserves a larger place in Dubbo's Circular Economy Roadmap?**

Also ask for the current status of the Whylandra reuse-shop/master-plan work.

# Research conclusion

Dubbo's strongest circular economy already exists in businesses that make money by **keeping valuable things useful**.

Its formal waste system is increasingly capable at **recovering materials**.

The gap between them is the opportunity:

> **A resident should not have to know the right repairer, charity, Marketplace group or specialist before an item can avoid becoming waste.**

The clearest next phase is therefore not another generic recycling campaign. It is to test the missing decision point:

**Can we identify and redirect reusable/repairable products before they cross into the waste stream, safely and economically?**
