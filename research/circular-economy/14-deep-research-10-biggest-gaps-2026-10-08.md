# Deep Research Answers — 10 Biggest Dubbo Circular-Economy Gaps

**Research date:** 8 October 2026  
**Scope:** Dubbo, NSW. Circular economy, e-waste, repair, reuse, Repair Café, Library of Things, charities, C&D and local coordination.  
**Status:** current public-source research plus reconciliation against the existing DubboEwaste research corpus.

## Method and evidence rule

This report answers the ten highest-value questions identified after reviewing the current DubboEwaste research, historical DadLAN device-sourcing work and related refurb-market work.

The rule is deliberately strict:

- **VERIFIED LOCAL** — current Dubbo-specific primary/official evidence.
- **VERIFIED COMPARATOR** — strong Australian/NSW evidence, but not Dubbo data.
- **PARTLY ANSWERED** — enough evidence to narrow the question materially, but an important local fact still needs direct evidence.
- **NOT PUBLICLY ANSWERABLE** — no responsible answer can be produced from current public information. The missing evidence is stated explicitly.
- **INFERENCE** — a planning conclusion from evidence, not a measured local fact.

Negative search results mean “not found in the public sources reviewed”, not proof that an unpublished activity does not exist.

# Executive answer

The ten gaps are no longer equal.

Four are now **substantially answered**:

1. Council really has planned reuse-shop / Circular Economy Hub infrastructure at Whylandra, although implementation remains unverified.
2. A real regional circular-economy coordinating nucleus now exists: Council says it is part of the newly-created Dubbo Region Circular Economy Hub.
3. Dubbo has real repair/reuse capacity and a credible host ecosystem for a Repair Café and Library of Things.
4. Dubbo’s C&D stream is enormous and local contractors already salvage/recycle, but product-level reuse is not measured.

The other six are “last-mile evidence” questions that web research cannot honestly close:

- condition of discarded bulky goods;
- exact e-waste downstream chain;
- actual annual retired-computer volume;
- actual Dubbo Repair Café demand;
- actual Dubbo Library-of-Things demand;
- charity hardgoods reject/unsold routes and repair economics.

The next stage should therefore be **measured local pilots, information requests and structured interviews**, not another broad internet search.

# 1. How much reusable stuff is actually being thrown away in Dubbo?

## Answer: NOT PUBLICLY ANSWERABLE YET — but the scale and benchmark are now much clearer

### What is verified locally

Dubbo Regional Council’s pre-booked bulky rubbish collection has operated for two years and had completed **5,765 collections** by May 2026. In 2024/25 there were 2,990 bookings; Council reported more than 2,000 bookings in the first half of 2025/26. Around 21,500 residences were eligible for the service at the January 2026 checkpoint.

Council says the booked model increases landfill diversion because staff have more time to separate recyclable from non-recyclable materials.

Current collection allowance is up to roughly **2 m³ / one box-trailer load** per eligible service.

None of those public Council sources provides a **condition audit** showing:

- working as-is;
- minor repair;
- major repair;
- donor-parts;
- material-recycling only;
- landfill only.

Therefore there is still no defensible Dubbo percentage for “reusable goods thrown away”.

### Best NSW benchmark

The 2023 SSROC/APC regional bulky-waste audit examined more than 100 tonnes of kerbside clean-up material. It found:

- furniture: **28.8% by weight**;
- wood: 12.2%;
- plastics: 6.7%;
- metal: 6.4%;
- whitegoods/electrical: 5.4%;
- e-waste: 5.3%;
- about **58% by weight was recyclable or potentially reusable**;
- about 34% was landfill-only.

This is a Sydney-region comparator, **not a Dubbo estimate**. It demonstrates that a condition/diversion audit can reveal a large recoverable fraction, but it cannot be multiplied across Dubbo’s collections as if the populations and collection systems were equivalent.

### Strongest conclusion

The original question cannot be answered numerically without field data. The strongest research-backed action is a **Dubbo bulky-waste condition audit**, ideally embedded in the booked collection system rather than manually scavenging at Whylandra.

A useful sample is 300–500 individual objects across multiple collection days, coded:

1. working;
2. clean/reusable as-is;
3. minor repair under 30 minutes / low-cost part;
4. repairable but economically difficult;
5. parts donor;
6. recyclable material;
7. residual landfill;
8. hazardous/prohibited.

Also record product category, estimated weight/volume and whether a known Dubbo reuse/repair outlet exists.

# 3. Where does Dubbo e-waste actually go now?

## Answer: THE PUBLIC CHAIN STILL BREAKS AFTER FIRST COLLECTION

### Council route

Council’s adopted Waste Strategy recorded the former **Matthews Metals** e-waste collection arrangement as commencing 30 June 2023 and expiring **30 June 2025**.

The current Council GIPA contracts register only has to disclose contracts meeting the statutory register criteria (including the $150,000 threshold described by Council). No current public record reviewed identifies the post-June-2025 e-waste contractor.

Council still accepts domestic e-waste free at Whylandra, so the service continues even though the current commercial/contractual pathway is not publicly named.

### AMR route

The old Matthews site at **34 Mountbatten Drive, Dubbo** is now operated/publicly branded by Australian Metal Recycling (AMR), which describes Dubbo as formerly Matthews Metal Management.

AMR currently says:
- electronic waste is accepted;
- computers, monitors, printers and related electrical equipment are e-waste;
- the Dubbo yard is part of its NSW network;
- lithium batteries are excluded from its accepted-material list.

What AMR does **not** publish is the first receiving processor/facility for Dubbo e-waste after it leaves the yard.

### Candidate processors are not proof

NetWaste says Sims collects e-waste from the majority of member councils but also says some councils have individual arrangements.

Sircel, ACE and Sims are all credible NSW/regional e-waste processors. Existing repo forensic research found no direct evidence linking a current AMR Dubbo load to any one of them.

Do not write:

- “AMR Dubbo sends e-waste to Sircel Parkes”;
- “AMR Dubbo sends it to Sims”;
- “AMR Dubbo sends it to Rutherford”;
- “Council now uses AMR”;

unless a current docket, contract, certificate, manifest or written answer establishes it.

### What closes this question

One of:
- Council’s current purchase order/RFQ/contract;
- AMR dispatch docket;
- certificate of recycling;
- downstream invoice/manifest;
- written AMR compliance response;
- current co-regulator partner record.

# 4. How many usable computers are retiring in Dubbo every year?

## Answer: NO DUBBO TOTAL EXISTS PUBLICLY; NEW STATEWIDE EVIDENCE SHOWS THE SUPPLY CLASS IS LARGE

### New NSW Government benchmark

The NSW Device Bank procurement Q&A says the 12-month pilot expected:

- about **2,800 laptops already in storage** at the start;
- about **2,500 more laptops** during the 12-month term;
- approximately **5,300 government laptops** in total;
- mostly HP x360 1030 G3/G4, with some G7/G8;
- most expected to be **five years old or older**.

The May 2026 NSW Device Bank announcement confirms the objective is to refurbish surplus government laptops and redistribute them for digital inclusion rather than send them directly to e-waste.

This is the clearest current quantitative proof that usable/refurbishable enterprise laptops exist at meaningful scale in NSW.

It is **not a Dubbo allocation** and should not be divided by population to invent one.

### NSW schools

The Department of Education says it has formalised school e-waste collection and publicly acknowledged a **backlog of schools requesting collection**, with “the vendor” working through that backlog.

Current school procedure requires lifecycle management, stocktakes and disposal of unsupported equipment. The public material does not name the vendor or publish Dubbo/Western NSW batch volumes.

### Other Dubbo institutions

Existing research has identified disposal frameworks/leads for:
- Charles Sturt University;
- TAFE NSW;
- Western NSW Local Health District/eHealth NSW;
- Council;
- schools;
- large local businesses.

But current annual retired-device counts and last-batch outcomes are not public for Dubbo.

Historical CSU evidence shows donation has happened, but old donation counts are not a current Dubbo fleet-flow measure.

### Best defensible answer

There is strong evidence of a substantial NSW institutional retirement stream and explicit evidence of school e-waste backlog, but **the annual usable-computer retirement volume in Dubbo remains unknown**.

It is now a records/interview problem, not a web-research problem.

For each top institution request:
- active laptop/desktop fleet count;
- normal refresh cycle;
- devices retired in FY2024/25 and FY2025/26;
- make/model/age distribution;
- outcome of last completed batch;
- number reused/redeployed/donated/sold/recycled/destroyed;
- current provider;
- next expected batch.

# 5. Would Dubbo actually use a Repair Café?

## Answer: THE AUSTRALIAN MODEL HAS STRONG DEMAND; DUBBO DEMAND IS STILL UNMEASURED

No operating formal Dubbo Repair Café was found in current public research.

That means we cannot honestly answer “yes, X people per month in Dubbo” before a trial.

But the external evidence is now strong enough to say a pilot is reasonable rather than speculative.

### Australian evidence

RepairMonitor’s Australian dashboard had **8,788 recorded repairs across 20 participating Australian Repair Cafés** by late September 2026.

Productivity Commission repair evidence records:
- one council’s Repair Café visitors growing from about 20/session in 2016 to 44/session in 2019;
- items presented growing from about 20/session to 65/session across the same period;
- reported success rates of around 70–80% for some Australian Repair Cafés;
- lower success for electronics/computers than textiles and simpler mechanical items.

Current examples reinforce the pattern:
- Waverley’s first event repaired **107 of 118 items** and reported 116 kg diverted;
- Surf Coast reported 505 items repaired during 2025 plus advice on another 113, with 600+ visitors across the year;
- Pascoe Vale reported 558 successful repairs from 665 requests in its 2024/25 annual report.

These are not forecasts for Dubbo.

### What the evidence suggests for Dubbo

A broad first event with 50 items would create unnecessary volunteer/safety risk. The existing repo recommendation of **6–8 pre-booked owner-assisted computer/device sessions** is a sensible demand experiment precisely because local demand is unknown.

For a broader Repair Café pilot, collect:
- bookings attempted;
- no-shows;
- walk-ins;
- item type;
- repair outcome;
- minutes;
- part needed;
- commercial referral;
- what owner would have done otherwise;
- postcode/suburb only if useful and privacy-minimised;
- volunteer hours;
- safety exclusions.

Run at least three sessions before concluding there is or is not demand.

# 6. Would a Dubbo Library of Things / Tool Library work?

## Answer: NO DUBBO DEMAND DATA YET, BUT A LOW-COST PILOT HAS A CREDIBLE LOCAL HOST MODEL

This was the least-developed gap in the repo.

### No current Dubbo service found

No current public Macquarie Regional Library material reviewed lists a general Library of Things or tool-lending service.

Dubbo Library currently publishes:
- free library membership;
- public computers/Wi-Fi;
- meeting rooms;
- Connect Room;
- Makers Club;
- STEAM Club;
- Tech Help;
- community meeting rooms with **no fee for not-for-profit groups**.

This means the library already has a membership/loan culture, physical branch, community programming and NFP space. It does not mean the library has agreed to run a Library of Things.

### Australian operating models

**Sydney Library of Things**
- annual membership $85 / $60 concession;
- up to five items;
- two-week loans;
- online reservation via myTurn;
- liability waiver;
- fees fund maintenance, insurance and inventory.

**Public-library model**
Australian public libraries such as Myli and Noosa show that non-book objects can be incorporated into library lending. Myli requires adult membership, booking/confirmation, staffed pickup/return and item checks on return.

**Community-cooperative model**
Other Australian libraries of things use membership/volunteer commitments, waivers, test-and-tag regimes and donated inventory.

### Recommended Dubbo design to test

Do **not** start with chainsaws, ladders or high-risk power tools.

Start with 20–40 low-risk/high-use items:
- stud finder;
- basic hand-tool kits;
- socket set;
- bike-maintenance tools;
- sewing machine only if maintenance/training can be supported;
- carpet/upholstery cleaner only if hygiene/process is solved;
- cake tins / party equipment;
- camping equipment;
- telescope/binoculars;
- simple gardening hand tools;
- thermal camera / energy monitor;
- repair-tool kits;
- portable projectors / creative kits if compatible with library programming.

Two candidate structures:

**A. Library-hosted pilot**
MRL owns/loans items through its normal system after governance/insurance review.

**B. Community group co-located at library**
A separate incorporated/NFP body owns the objects and uses library meeting/community space for scheduled pickup/return.

B is organisationally easier to test without assuming the library can immediately absorb tool liability into its normal lending policy.

### What must be measured first

A 100–200 response local survey should ask residents to rank exact objects, not just “would you use a Library of Things?”

Then pre-register the first 20 items based on:
- purchase/donation cost;
- expected uses/year;
- retail replacement/hire cost avoided;
- inspection burden;
- cleaning burden;
- injury risk;
- storage volume;
- consumables;
- loss rate;
- specialist training needed.

# 8. What does repair actually cost in Dubbo, and why does repair lose to replacement?

## Answer: REPAIR SUPPLY EXISTS; LOCAL PRICE/FAILURE DATA IS TOO PATCHY FOR A CITY-WIDE ECONOMIC CURVE

Current local repairers clearly demonstrate a functioning market, but public prices are inconsistent.

### Current evidence

Wheeler Cycles publishes:
- Level 1 service: **$130 plus parts**;
- Level 2 service: **$190 plus parts**.

This is valuable because it shows the labour/service floor for a skilled local repair can already be a substantial share of the replacement price of a low-end bicycle.

Right Click Go advertises computer repair with **No Fix, No Pay**, but does not expose a simple public numeric rate.

Case Indulgence’s current Dubbo pages use model-specific assessments/quotes and explicitly say repair-vs-replace should consider:
- age;
- software support;
- multiple simultaneous faults;
- frame/secondary damage;
- parts availability;
- equivalent replacement cost;
- data/accessory/setup value.

This is a much more realistic economic rule than “repair if part costs under X”.

### What is still missing

For Dubbo, no public dataset tells us:
- diagnostic fee by trade;
- median labour charge;
- typical part markup;
- percentage fixed;
- percentage technically repairable but economically declined;
- customer approval rate after quote;
- parts wait time;
- repeat/warranty burden.

### Practical repair-economics model

For a resident-owned item, compare:

**repair total**
= diagnosis/call-out + labour + parts + travel + expected repeat-risk

against:

**replacement-equivalent cost**
= equivalent new/refurb replacement + setup/data migration/accessories + disposal costs - resale/salvage value of old item.

A Repair Café changes this equation by donating diagnostic/simple-repair labour, but it should not undercut complex work that appropriately belongs with local commercial repairers.

### Local research that would close it

Interview at least:
- 3 computer/phone repairers;
- 2 appliance repair contacts;
- 2 bike/tool/mower repairers;
- 1 furniture/restoration operator;
- 1 clothing/shoe repairer.

Ask for ranges and ratios, not commercially sensitive customer records.

# 9. How much of Dubbo’s C&D waste could remain a product rather than become recycled material?

## Answer: C&D IS THE BIGGEST TONNAGE OPPORTUNITY, BUT PRODUCT-LEVEL SALVAGE IS NOT MEASURED

### Scale

Council’s adopted Waste Strategy reports **38,525 tonnes/year of C&D waste for sorting/disposal** in its baseline generation table. C&D overall is around 43% of the approximately 100,926 tonnes/year managed in the strategy reference period.

This means tiny percentage improvements can represent large tonnages.

### Existing local circular activity

Boland Contracting currently says its Dubbo demolition work:
- reuses, recycles and salvages materials where possible;
- handles wood, carpets, plasterboard, roofing, insulation, tiles;
- salvages brick, porcelain and concrete where possible;
- uses selective demolition where components such as doors, windows or architectural items can be recovered;
- crushes concrete/masonry into reusable aggregate.

Tubba-Gah Galamaay Contracting also advertises demolition with salvage, recycling and documented waste management.

This proves the private market is not simply “demolish everything to landfill”.

### The important distinction

**Material recovery**:
- crush concrete;
- scrap steel;
- chip/process material.

**Product reuse**:
- remove and resell/reuse a door;
- retain hardwood beams;
- recover windows;
- keep cabinetry;
- sell reusable bricks/tiles;
- recover fixtures.

Council’s current data does not quantify that second category, and no clearly established Dubbo building-material reuse warehouse was found in this research.

### Best opportunity

The next research should be a **deconstruction/material-reuse pilot**, not another generic recycling study.

For 5–10 upcoming demolition/strip-out projects record:
- tonnes by material;
- items salvaged as products;
- items sent to material recycling;
- landfill;
- labour added by selective deconstruction;
- sale/reuse value;
- avoided disposal/haulage;
- storage/logistics constraint.

Council’s Waste Strategy already includes C&D segregation guidance as an action, creating a policy hook for this.

# 10. Who is actually going to coordinate Dubbo’s circular economy?

## Answer: COUNCIL + THE NEW DUBBO REGION CIRCULAR ECONOMY HUB ARE THE STRONGEST CURRENT COORDINATING NUCLEUS

This question has changed materially.

### Current Council evidence

Council’s July–December 2025 progress report says:

- Council is part of the **Dubbo Region Circular Economy Hub**;
- named participants include **Taronga Western Plains Zoo, Fletchers International and Alkane**;
- Council was holding ongoing roundtable discussions with the Newcastle Institute for Energy and Resources on circular solutions for wind-turbine blades.

The same period reported the Dubbo Solar Panel Recycling Innovation Hub project as a shortlisted regional-investment initiative.

### Waste Strategy governance actions

The adopted Waste Strategy separately calls for:

- **Action 3.6:** formation/participation in a **Dubbo Region Circularity Cooperative**, including a website, education/promotion, working groups and grant applications;
- **Action 3.7:** a regional **Circular Economy Roadmap**;
- **Action 3.13:** generation of a **Circular Economy and Strategic Projects Position**.

### What remains unclear

The public material reviewed does not establish:
- whether “Dubbo Region Circular Economy Hub” and “Circularity Cooperative” are the same governance body;
- charter/legal structure;
- chair/secretariat;
- complete membership;
- public joining mechanism;
- meeting frequency/minutes;
- project pipeline;
- published Circular Economy Roadmap;
- whether Repair Café, Library of Things, charity reuse, Whylandra reuse-shop or IT reuse sit in its remit.

### Practical conclusion

Do **not** create a competing “coordination body” before talking to this Hub.

The sensible model is:

**Council / Dubbo Region Circular Economy Hub**
→ regional strategy, industry, grants, major material flows

**community reuse network / Repair Café / Library of Things**
→ household participation, skills, sharing and repair

**DubboEwaste / ITAD/refurb partners**
→ secure electronics reuse and chain-of-custody

**charities / repair businesses / Men's Sheds**
→ existing local reuse and skills network

The research gap is now **governance connection**, not “who could possibly lead?”

# Highest-value experiments now

## Experiment A — bulky goods condition audit
Do this first if Council cooperation is available. It gives the city its first product-condition evidence rather than only tonnage/recycling data.

## Experiment B — three-session Repair Café
Keep it booked, safe and measurable. Do not infer demand from Facebook likes or survey enthusiasm.

## Experiment C — Library of Things demand + 20-item pilot
Survey exact objects, then borrow/use data should decide expansion.

## Experiment D — 20–30 computer retirement pilot
Known-authority devices only. Measure reuse rate, labour, sanitisation outcomes, resale/donation value and residual recycling.

## Experiment E — C&D deconstruction sample
Measure product salvage separately from material recycling.

# Evidence discipline

This report deliberately leaves some questions open.

Those remaining gaps are **not research failures**. They are facts held by organisations or facts that do not yet exist until someone measures them.

The project should now distinguish:

- **web-verifiable policy/service facts**;
- **organisation-held operational facts**;
- **commercial/private facts**;
- **new local evidence that requires a pilot**.

That distinction is more valuable than forcing every row to say “answered”.
