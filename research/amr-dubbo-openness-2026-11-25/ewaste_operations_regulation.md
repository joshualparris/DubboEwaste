## Q2. Is AMR an NTCRS collection point or recycler? Which co-regulatory arrangement? Who processes AMR's e-waste downstream?

### Takeaway
I found no evidence that AMR, at any site, is an NTCRS collection point or AS/NZS 5377-certified recycler, and no named downstream processor. How the scheme is structured does matter. If AMR's e-waste flows into an NTCRS co-regulatory arrangement, that arrangement funds collection and recycling and reports recycled weight against its targets. Josh can only confirm this by asking AMR directly, or by searching the TechCollect, E-Cycle Solutions or Ecycle locators by postcode.

### Cited Findings
- The NTCRS was established in 2011. It gives households and small businesses free access to industry-funded collection and recycling. Liable importers and manufacturers join approved co-regulatory arrangements, which are "responsible for day-to-day operation … including organising collection and recycling of e-waste on behalf of liable party members" — [NTCRS overview (nationalepa.gov.au)](https://www.nationalepa.gov.au/node/282)
- Co-regulatory arrangements "publicise and organise NTCRS collection and recycling activities" and report outcomes to the regulator. NTCRS collection points provide free disposal for households and small businesses. "E-waste disposal outside of NTCRS services may incur a fee" — [NTCRS roles and responsibilities](https://www.nationalepa.gov.au/node/420)
- Each co-regulatory arrangement is responsible for achieving its members' share of the targets: reasonable access, annual recycling targets and a 90% material recovery target — [Co-regulatory arrangements – NTCRS](https://www.nationalepa.gov.au/node/269)
- The Product Stewardship Online portal supports "the government approved co-regulatory arrangements that deliver television and computer recycling services" — [ps.awe.gov.au](https://ps.awe.gov.au)
- Businesses with a small number of computers can use the scheme free at a collection point ("only small quantities will be accepted"). Commercial recyclers outside the scheme charge fees — [Recycling Near You – computers for workplaces](https://recyclingnearyou.com.au/material/work/computers)
- An example of a reuse channel inside a collection network: Victorians can donate functioning computers to participating Vinnies stores, where trained staff test them — [Recycling Near You – computers for workplaces](https://recyclingnearyou.com.au/material/work/computers)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] NTCRS information and the contact address (ewaste@nationalepa.gov.au) now appear on nationalepa.gov.au. That suggests administration has moved to the new national EPA. I haven't verified this formally, and the older DCCEEW and agriculture.gov.au documents may be outdated in places.
- [INFERENCE] If AMR Dubbo is an NTCRS collection point, its NTCRS-collected TVs and computers would normally go to the arrangement's contracted recycler, who counts their weight. AMR's contract with that arrangement or recycler (if one exists) could restrict diverting those items. Items AMR buys or receives outside the scheme, such as commercial or scrap-value loads, would be far less constrained.

### Gaps

- AMR's downstream e-waste processor is unknown.

## Q3. NTCRS rules on reuse: can scheme-collected products be diverted to reuse? Does reuse count? Ownership, weight reporting and audit? Reform status?

### Takeaway
The NTCRS rules are built around recycling, not reuse. The 2021 Rules (as made) define "recycle" as material recovery, require recycling by an AS/NZS 5377-certified recycler, and never mention reuse or refurbishment. The 2014 reporting methodology says outright that reused or refurbished products don't count towards the material recovery target. Nothing in the Rules addresses who owns collected products, but the arrangements face chain-of-custody, record-keeping and audit obligations. Those obligations plausibly make collection points cautious about handing scheme items to a third party. A 2025 evaluation recommended tighter oversight of downstream flows. A broader e-waste scheme (small electricals and solar) has been under development since 2022–23, but I couldn't confirm its status as of Nov 2026.

### Cited Findings
- Recycling and Waste Reduction (Product Stewardship—Televisions and Computers) Rules 2021 (as made, 25 May 2021):
  - s 4 defines "recycle" as "process the product for the purpose of recovering usable materials, and includes disassemble or shred the product". The words "reuse" and "refurbish" don't appear anywhere in the instrument — [Rules 2021, as made](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
  - s 7(1)(f): recycling must be done "by a person certified to AS/NZS 5377" at a certified facility. s 7(1)(g) and s 7(2): products must be "comprehensively recycled" — [Rules 2021](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
  - s 16: "The material recovery target is 90% of television or computer products, based on weight". s 11 and s 15 cover recycling targets, carry-forward of excess and the shortfall penalty into the next year — [Rules 2021](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
  - s 7(3): collection must be free for households and small businesses and accept every kind of covered product — [Rules 2021](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
  - No provision covers ownership or transfer of title of collected products — [Rules 2021](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
  - s 22–23: annual audit by a registered company auditor covering performance against each outcome. s 26: the administrator must keep accurate records (technical data, certifications, inspection records) for 5 years — [Rules 2021](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00624/asmade/2021-05-25/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
- Material Recovery Measurement and Reporting Methodology (Equilibrium, June 2014) [OLDER]:
  - "any re-use, refurbishment and/or re-marketing of televisions and computers is not considered material recovery and does not count towards the target"
  - "Products sent for refurbishment, re-use and/or re-marketing cannot be counted towards the material recovery target"
  - "Where whole products are re-used, these materials are not counted towards the material recovery target"
  - The methodology "is based on chain-of-custody tracking and tracing of materials", and administrators must document "the disposition of the material to a market" — [Material recovery methodology (PDF)](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/material-recovery-measurement-reporting-methodology.pdf)
- The 2021 NTCRS Compliance Framework (Aug 2021) sets out the regulator's compliance and enforcement approach and includes a tip-off channel — [NTCRS Compliance Framework](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/ntcrs-compliance-framework_0.pdf)
- The 2025 NTCRS evaluation (posted 24 June 2025) assessed whether to "expand to include other e-waste products". Its recommendations include reviewing whether service levels "provide equitable access to the scheme for consumers in rural, regional and remote locations" and to "Increase and enhance oversight of the downstream flow of recycled products". The summary page doesn't mention reuse, refurbishment or data security — [APO: Evaluation of the NTCRS (2025)](https://apo.org.au/node/331180)
- The 2020 review of the Product Stewardship Act 2011 recommended expanding the NTCRS to "all electrical and electronic products with a plug or battery" [OLDER] — [Waste Management Review](https://wastemanagementreview.com.au/fed-govt-releases-product-stewardship-act-review/)
- DCCEEW consulted on regulating small electrical and electronic equipment and solar PV ("e-products"). The consultation closed 23 July 2023 — [DCCEEW consultation hub](https://consult.dcceew.gov.au/regulation-small-electrical-products-solar-pv)
- The proposal covers small electrical and electronic equipment up to 20 kg plus solar PV, implemented under the Recycling and Waste Reduction Act 2020 (IEA policy page, last updated 18 Oct 2024) — [IEA](https://www.iea.org/policies/25113-regulation-for-small-electrical-products-and-solar-photovoltaic-systems)
- In Jan 2026 the Australian Government funded a national solar panel recycling pilot (AU$24.7m) — [pv-tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/australian-government-commits-au24-7-million-to-national-solar-module-recycling-pilot/); [pv magazine, 23 Jan 2026](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/01/23/australia-to-fund-national-solar-panel-recycling-pilot/)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Under the current rules, a laptop diverted to reuse earns nothing for the arrangement or recycler: it adds no recycled weight. An AMR yard paid per kg, or measured by an arrangement on weight, has a mild financial reason not to divert scheme items. Diverting 10–20 machines is immaterial to tonnages, though. The bigger brake is probably contractual and chain-of-custody paperwork, not the targets.
- [INFERENCE] Because reuse isn't recognised, nothing in the Rules prohibits reuse outright. The constraint would come from (a) the collection point's contract with its arrangement or recycler, and (b) the arrangement's need to account for what it collects. That points the trial towards non-NTCRS stock: commercial or bought-in loads, or items AMR receives outside a scheme service. Failing that, it needs written sign-off from the arrangement.
- [INFERENCE] The 2025 evaluation's push for more downstream-flow oversight suggests the scheme is getting stricter on traceability, not looser.
- [INFERENCE] The rules cited are the as-made 2021 version and a 2014 methodology. Later compilations or guidance may have changed details.

### Gaps
- I couldn't confirm whether the expanded e-products scheme (small electricals, solar PV) or any NTCRS rule change had been legislated or started by Nov 2026.
- I couldn't confirm whether any current (2024–26) NTCRS guidance recognises reuse, for example through an AS/NZS 5377 reuse clause or a reuse credit.
- I didn't find the standard contract terms between arrangements (TechCollect, E-Cycle Solutions and others) and their collection points, such as title or diversion clauses.

## Q4. NSW rules: landfill ban, EPA licensing, whether taking items to a home shed triggers "waste" or licensing issues, resource recovery exemptions

### Takeaway
As at Nov 2026, NSW has no statewide e-waste landfill ban. A 2025 private member's bill to create one was negatived and lapsed (WA and Victoria do have bans). The relevant NSW frame is the POEO Act. Waste storage, processing and resource recovery are licensable "scheduled activities" above set thresholds, and transporting waste to a place that can't lawfully take it is an offence (s 143). 2025 amendments may bring stored lithium-ion waste batteries under hazardous waste storage licensing. The open question for Josh is whether laptops leaving AMR are legally "waste". If so, a home shed is not a lawful waste facility.

### Cited Findings
- The Protection of the Environment Operations Amendment (e-Waste) Bill 2025 was a private member's bill (Gareth Ward) to "prevent e-waste being sent to landfill and to require e-waste to be recycled". It was introduced 28 March 2025, negatived by the Legislative Assembly on 18 Sept 2025 and lapsed 28 Sept 2025 — [NSW Parliament bill details](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/bills/Pages/bill-details.aspx?pk=18735)
- The bill's explanatory note states: "Under the Act, section 143 it is also an offence to transport waste to a place that cannot lawfully be used as a waste facility for the waste." The bill would also have made recycling a mandatory licence condition for e-waste processing — [Bill first print (PDF)](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/bill/files/18735/First%20Print.pdf)
- For comparison, WA banned e-waste to landfill from 1 July 2024. Households aren't obligated under the WA ban, and its regulations reference AS 5377:2022 for re-use or recycling — [WA Government e-waste ban](https://www.wa.gov.au/service/building-utilities-and-essential-services/waste-management/e-waste-landfill-ban-wa); [WA ban page](https://wa.gov.au/service/building-utilities-and-essential-services/waste-management/e-waste-landfill-ban)
- One industry blog claims that from 1 July 2019 e-waste is "no longer accepted at landfills across Australia". This is overbroad: the 2019 ban was Victorian, and the NSW bill above failed — [Iron Mountain blog](https://resources.ironmountain.com/en-gb/blogs-and-articles/a/australian-e-waste-legislation-and-the-impact-on-your-business), contradicted by [NSW Parliament](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/bills/Pages/bill-details.aspx?pk=18735)
- EPA licences are issued under the POEO Act. Waste resource recovery, waste processing and waste storage are licensable activities with thresholds — [NSW EPA guide to licensing fact sheet](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/150105-guide-to-licensing-factsheet.pdf)
- The POEO (Waste) Regulation 2014 sets the thresholds at which processing, recovery and storage of waste require an environment protection licence — [NSW EPA reduced licensing thresholds](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/your-environment/waste/waste-overview/licensing/reduced-licensing-thresholds)
- Environmental Legislation Amendment Act 2025: "The change may mean that a licence for the scheduled activity of 'waste storage' will be required where lithium ion waste batteries are being stored and the thresholds that relate to hazardous waste are met (Schedule 1 clause 42)". It also added unlicensed waste storage or disposal to the repeat waste offences list — [NSW EPA, ELA Act 2025 page (search snippet)](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/Working-together/For-local-government/environmental-legislation-amendment-act-2025)
- The POEO (Waste) Regulation defines "resource recovery exemption" as an exemption granted under clause 91 (authorised by clause 92) — [POEO (Waste) Regulation 2014](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/2018-11-16/sl-2014-0666)
- A 2023 regulation provides that waste storage "is not a scheduled activity in certain circumstances relating to community recycling centres and household chemical clean-out events" — [sl 2023-72](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/pdf/asmade/sl-2023-72)
- NSW passed a Product Lifecycle Responsibility Act 2025, referenced in EPA "What's new in law" alongside new executive liability offences — [NSW EPA What's new in law](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/Licensing-and-Regulation/Legislation-and-compliance/whats-new-in-law)
- NSW battery reform (EPA media release, 7 Apr 2026): the regulation applies to small and removable batteries under 5 kg, including AA, AAA, power banks and e-bike/e-scooter batteries — [NSW EPA media release (search snippet)](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/news/epamedia/260407-nsw-leads-the-country-in-battery-reform-to-fight-fires-and-pollution)
- Dubbo's Whylandra Waste and Recycling Centre operates under EPL 6058 — [Dubbo Regional Council](https://www.dubbo.nsw.gov.au/Households-Residents/Rubbish-Recycling-and-Sustainability/whylandra-waste-recycling-centre)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE, background knowledge not re-verified this session] The POEO Act Dictionary defines "waste" broadly. It covers discarded, rejected, unwanted or surplus substances, including those intended for recycling, reprocessing or reuse, and something can still be waste even if it will be recovered. A laptop handed to a scrap yard for recycling is very likely "waste" in AMR's hands. Before relying on this, check the Dictionary in the current POEO Act.
- [INFERENCE] The risky step is AMR transferring waste to Josh's home shed (s 143), plus any storage or processing there. At 10–20 units this is almost certainly far below licensing thresholds. s 143, though, is about the destination being a lawful waste facility, not about volume. Ways to lower the risk:
  - (a) Treat the selected units as goods, not waste: AMR selects or sorts them as reusable before they're processed, and title passes to Josh or a community organisation in writing as a donation or sale of second-hand equipment.
  - (b) Return rejects to AMR quickly and keep minimal quantities on site, especially of loose lithium-ion batteries.
  - (c) Ask NSW EPA (Environment Line) directly whether a small reuse pilot needs any authorisation or exemption.
  - Separately from the EPA, the home-shed site may raise zoning or tenancy questions with council or the landlord.
- [INFERENCE] With no NSW landfill ban, AMR has no state-law reason to refuse reuse diversion. The pressures come from NTCRS and contracts, liability and its brand.
- [INFERENCE] Josh's earlier "authorised reuse pilot" framing with council fits the EPA's resource recovery order and exemption framework. I found no existing general exemption specific to e-waste reuse.

### Gaps
- I didn't get exact NSW licensing thresholds for waste storage or resource recovery of e-waste or hazardous waste (Li-ion) outside the "regulated area". The EPA pages didn't return their body text.
- I couldn't confirm whether lithium-ion battery waste is now formally classified as hazardous waste in NSW, or the commencement date of the ELA Act 2025 change.
- I didn't get details of the April 2026 NSW battery regulation: whether embedded laptop batteries are covered and what obligations it puts on collectors.
- I found no NSW resource recovery order or exemption specifically covering reuse of e-waste.

## Q5. Legal and liability factors a recycler would weigh: title, Privacy Act and APPs, ISM and NIST sanitisation, insurance, WHS, electrical safety

### Takeaway
A recycler's main concerns are likely to be:
1. Data: a leak from a device it was handed could damage it reputationally, and possibly legally if it's an APP entity.
2. Clear transfer of title and responsibility.
3. Liability for unsafe donated electrical goods.

APP 11.2 puts the duty to destroy or de-identify personal information on whichever APP entity holds it. NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (Sept 2025) replaced Rev. 1. NSW law covers the safety of second-hand electrical goods that are sold or supplied. I didn't research WHS for volunteers, insurance or AS/NZS 3760 in depth (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings
- APP 11.2: an APP entity must take reasonable steps to destroy or de-identify personal information it holds once it's no longer needed for any permitted purpose, unless it's required by law to retain it. APP 11 only applies to personal information an entity "holds" — [OAIC APP Guidelines Ch 11](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-11-app-11-security-of-personal-information); [OAIC Ch 11 v1.2 PDF](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1287/app-guidelines-chapter-11-v1.2.pdf)
- Reasonable steps include "technical and organisational measures" (new APP 11.3) — [OAIC Guide to securing personal information](https://www.oaic.gov.au/agencies-and-organisations/guides/guide-to-securing-personal-information)
- The OAIC is updating its guide to reflect the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. The guide isn't legally binding, but the OAIC refers to it when investigating — [OAIC guide (old page)](https://www.oaic.gov.au/_old/privacy/guidance-and-advice/guide-to-securing-personal-information); [OAIC guide](https://www.oaic.gov.au/agencies-and-organisations/guides/guide-to-securing-personal-information)
- The OAIC lists physically losing "computer equipment or portable storage devices containing personal information" as an example of a data breach scenario — [OAIC APP guidelines (old)](https://www.oaic.gov.au/_old/privacy/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-12-app-12-access-to-personal-information)
- NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2, Guidelines for Media Sanitization:
  - Published September 2025 (final 26 Sept 2025), superseding Rev. 1 (Dec 2014)
  - Focus shifted to "establishing an agency or enterprise media sanitization program"
  - An FAQ was published 16 July 2026
  - Sources: [NIST CSRC](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/88/r2/final); [NIST news](https://www.nist.gov/node/1887201); [NIST FAQ PDF](https://csrc.nist.gov/files/pubs/sp/800/88/r2/final/docs/sp800-88r2-faq.pdf)
- Rev. 2 keeps the Clear, Purge and Destroy methods and warns of disclosure risk when Clear is used on modern magnetic and flash media. The source is an erasure-software vendor, so treat it as secondary — [BitRaser article](https://www.bitraser.com/article/nist-800-88-r2-media-sanitization-guidelines.php)
- Blancco has published guidance on what Rev. 2 means for organisations aligning with NIST 800-88 (vendor source) — [Blancco blog](https://blancco.com/resources/blog-nist-800-88-rev-2-updated-standard/)
- NSW electrical safety:
  - New and second-hand products are covered by the Gas and Electricity (Consumer Safety) Act 2017 — [NSW Government – buying appliances](https://www.nsw.gov.au/topics/electrical-safety/buying-and-using-appliances)
  - "Organisations and individuals who sell or supply secondhand electrical equipment have legal obligations to ensure your safety". A second-hand article "must have been approved at the time of selling when new" — [NSW Government – buying and using electrical appliances](https://www.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-construction/safety-home/electrical-safety/buying-and-using-electrical-appliances)
  - Non-declared articles "must still" be safe and meet relevant Australian safety standards — [NSW Fair Trading Safe electrical goods](https://www.fairtrading.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/1084419/Safe-electrical-goods.pdf)
  - One NSW page also refers to an "Electricity (Consumer Safety) Act 2024". The naming conflicts with the 2017 Act references, so check which Act is current — [NSW Government – approval schemes](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/selling-goods-and-services/electrical-approval-schemes)
- Comparator only (Queensland, not NSW): sellers of second-hand in-scope electrical equipment don't have to test it but must tell buyers if it's untested — [WorkSafe Qld](https://www.worksafe.qld.gov.au/safety-and-prevention/hazards/electricity/electrical-products-and-equipment/selling-electrical-equipment/selling-second-hand)
- The 2025 NTCRS evaluation and industry commentary describe e-waste disposal as a growing cyber and data security concern. A PwC report calls e-waste "a critical cybersecurity issue" — [PwC device data sanitisation frameworks report](https://www.pwc.com.au/cyber/device-data-sanitisation-frameworks-report-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE, background knowledge not verified this session] Many small businesses (annual turnover of $3m or less) aren't APP entities, so whether AMR is bound by the APPs depends on its size and structure. Either way, a data leak traced to its yard would be a reputational risk. The donor organisations that discarded the devices hold the primary APP 11.2 obligation.
- [INFERENCE] For AMR, a written agreement should give the strongest reassurance. Points to cover:
  - (a) Title passes to Josh or the receiving entity when he collects.
  - (b) Josh carries responsibility for data sanitisation, with per-device Blancco certificates tied to serial numbers sent back to AMR.
  - (c) Drives that fail erasure are physically destroyed or returned to AMR flagged for destruction.
  - (d) Rejects come back to AMR within a set time.
  - (e) Josh indemnifies AMR and holds his own public liability cover.
