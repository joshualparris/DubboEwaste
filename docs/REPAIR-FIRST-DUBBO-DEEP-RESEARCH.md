# How do you get people in Dubbo to repair instead of replace?

**Deep research date:** 8 October 2026  
**Scope:** behaviour change, repair economics, local-government policy, community repair, electronics/appliances and Dubbo-specific implementation.  
**Project relevance:** Dubbo eWaste / circular economy / reuse-before-recycling.

---

# Executive answer

The strongest evidence does **not** support a strategy built mainly around telling people that repair is environmentally responsible.

People are much more likely to repair when repair is:

1. **cheap enough**
2. **easy to find**
3. **convenient and fast**
4. **trusted**
5. **low-risk**
6. **socially normal**
7. backed by **available parts, information and skilled repairers**.

The best Dubbo strategy is therefore a **bundle**, not one campaign:

> **Repair Bonus + trusted local repair network + regular Repair Café + free/cheap diagnosis + visible repair stories + parts/donor network + repair-first procurement and education.**

A repair voucher on its own can increase demand, but current research warns that subsidies without repair capacity can simply expose shortages of skilled repairers, spare parts and convenient services.

For Dubbo, the goal should be:

> **Make repair the default first check before replacement, not a niche hobby for technically confident people.**

---

# 1. Why people replace instead of repair

## 1.1 Price is the biggest immediate barrier

Research on willingness to pay for repair consistently finds repair price to be the most important factor.

A 2023 study of smartphones and washing machines found:
- repair price had the highest relative importance;
- repair time, travel/convenience and repair warranty/authorisation also materially affected willingness to pay;
- convenience becomes more important for higher-value products.

A 2025 WRAP/OC&C consumer study found consumers were generally willing to pay around **20% of a product's original value** for repair if the process was convenient. Their model estimated around **22%** for TVs, laptops, phones and whitegoods.

Other research puts household-appliance repair willingness broadly around **19–30% of replacement price**.

### Implication for Dubbo

A repair quote of:
- $250 on a $350 appliance will often lose to replacement;
- $80 on a $500 device has a much stronger chance.

The intervention therefore has to change the **effective price gap**, not only awareness.

---

## 1.2 Convenience and downtime matter almost as much as price

Consumers consider:
- how far they must travel;
- how hard it is to find the right repairer;
- whether they need multiple quotes;
- waiting time;
- whether they lose the phone/laptop/appliance for days or weeks;
- whether the repair is guaranteed.

Research on broken-electronics repair systems describes this as a broader **behavioural cost**.

The more inconvenient the repair journey is, the less people will pay to repair.

### Dubbo implication

A regional city has an advantage and a disadvantage.

Advantage:
- services can be locally networked and promoted simply.

Disadvantage:
- specialist repair capability and parts may not be available locally for every category.

The answer is not “educate people harder”.

It is:
- one directory;
- one intake/triage point;
- simple booking;
- transparent prices;
- rapid diagnosis;
- consolidated parts/logistics;
- loan devices where possible.

---

## 1.3 People often do not even consider repair

A study of replaced electronics found **60% of people replacing a defective product had not considered repair**.

People were especially unlikely to consider repair when the device was only partly malfunctioning rather than completely broken.

That is a crucial behaviour insight:

> The decision to replace often occurs **before anyone asks for a repair quote**.

### Dubbo implication

The behaviour target should be:

> **“Before you replace it, get one repair check.”**

That is easier to communicate than trying to turn everyone into DIY repairers.

---

## 1.4 Novelty and upgrades compete against repair

Repair is not competing only with the repair cost.

It competes with:
- a shiny new device;
- better features;
- phone upgrade plans;
- retailer finance;
- perceived status;
- “I deserve a new one”;
- fear that an older device will fail again soon.

Research on repair behaviour identifies **novelty seeking** as an important predictor of non-repair and short product lifetimes.

### Dubbo implication

Repair marketing must make repair feel like the **smart upgrade**, not the cheap/second-rate choice.

Messaging should be:

- “Keep the device you already like.”
- “Fix it for $X instead of replacing it for $Y.”
- “Same device. New battery. Another 2–3 years.”
- “Repair first. Replace only when the numbers do not make sense.”

Avoid moralising language like:
- “You are wasteful if you buy new.”

---

## 1.5 People do not trust repair outcomes

Consumers worry:

- Will the repair work?
- Will it break again?
- Will they be overcharged?
- Will a technician damage the item?
- Will their data be safe?
- Will the repair void a warranty?
- Will the repairer use poor-quality parts?

The Productivity Commission found substantial unnecessary barriers to repair in Australia and specifically highlighted:
- competition in repair markets;
- access to spare parts;
- access to repair information;
- consumer awareness of repair rights.

### Dubbo implication

Trust tools are essential:

- clear diagnosis;
- written quote;
- repair warranty;
- no surprise costs;
- data-handling statement;
- condition report;
- “not worth repairing” advice where appropriate.

A repair scheme loses credibility if customers think it exists to keep uneconomic devices alive at any cost.

---

# 2. Australia-specific evidence

## 2.1 Australia's repair problem is real

A 2026 Australian peer-reviewed study, **Examining the effectiveness of repair incentives in Australia**, concludes that Australia has:
- high e-waste generation;
- repair affordability barriers;
- limited repair venues;
- skills shortages;
- parts/information barriers;
- insufficient incentives.

The researchers argue that repair incentives can:
- make repair more affordable;
- reduce household spending;
- support local repair businesses;
- reduce e-waste;
- help build repair culture.

But they also warn:

> A repair bonus needs to be paired with investment in **repair capacity**, because Australia lacks the breadth/depth of repair businesses, repair cafés, trained technicians and volunteers needed for a large-scale shift.

That is particularly important in a regional city.

Source:
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14486563.2026.2667758

---

## 2.2 Australian repair-event data is encouraging

The same 2026 Australian study used repair-event data and reported approximate Australian outcomes of:

- **44% fixed**
- **21% repairable**
- **34% end-of-life**

for the analysed data.

Its Australian repair-barrier chart suggested a particularly large problem was simply **being unable to open/disassemble the product**, followed by:
- unavailable parts;
- unavailable repair information;
- lack of equipment;
- expensive parts.

That reinforces why a public campaign alone cannot fix the system.

---

## 2.3 Australians already repair phones when the path is clear

MobileMuster's consumer research says around **38% of Australians have repaired a mobile phone**.

Its research also identifies:
- independent repair shops;
- manufacturers;
- carriers;
- retailers

as existing repair routes.

MobileMuster also reports millions of unused phones are stored in homes, including many broken devices.

### Important 2026 example: Project Phoenix

MobileMuster/AMTA's Project Phoenix showed that phones with **low commercial resale value** could still be:
- securely wiped;
- professionally repaired/refurbished;
- redistributed for social benefit.

100 devices were refurbished and distributed through community partners.

This is a useful example for Dubbo:

> Repair economics are not always only about resale profit. Some borderline devices can create social value.

Sources:
- https://www.mobilemuster.com.au/repair/
- https://www.mobilemuster.com.au/reviving-underutilised-phones-through-refurbishment-pilot-outcomes/

# 3. Financial incentives: what actually works?

## 3.1 Repair vouchers / repair bonuses

The clearest international intervention is a repair bonus.

### Vienna

Vienna used vouchers covering **50% of repair cost**, originally capped around €100.

Research summarised in the 2026 Australian repair-incentives paper reports:
- more than **22,000 items repaired in the first year**;
- about **90% successfully repaired**;
- roughly two-thirds were electrical;
- the model contributed to Austria moving to a national repair-bonus program.

### Austria

Austria's nationwide scheme was used hundreds of thousands of times, with over **840,000 vouchers paid out between April 2022 and January 2024**, according to sources reviewed in the Australian paper.

### France

France's **Bonus Réparation** gives consumers a discount directly on repair invoices through participating accredited repairers.

Its stated objective is explicit:

> encourage repair instead of replacement and extend product life.

Sources:
- https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/politiques-publiques/bonus-reparation-quest-ce-que-cest
- Kariyawasama et al. 2026

---

## 3.2 Vouchers work, but they are not magic

A 2026 Journal of Cleaner Production study concluded:

- vouchers increased repair demand;
- awareness/willingness improved;
- but repair-business investment did not automatically follow;
- structural problems like repair skills, high costs and product design remained.

Another subsidy modelling study found repair subsidies can simultaneously:
- increase consumer demand;
- increase repair-company profitability;

but scheme design matters.

### Dubbo implication

Do not create a voucher program unless there are repairers able to take the work.

The order should be:

1. map repair capacity;
2. recruit repairers;
3. agree quality/warranty/claim standards;
4. launch incentive;
5. measure demand and capacity;
6. expand.

# 4. NSW is already testing the idea

This is no longer just a European concept.

The NSW EPA has funded the Southern Sydney Regional Organisation of Councils (SSROC) **$393,328** for:

> **Let's Fix It Sydney — Mapping Repair Incentives for Southern Sydney**

The project is:
- researching repair incentives;
- analysing overseas schemes;
- piloting a rebate system across 12 councils.

The Phase 1 work examined **16 repair-incentive schemes across seven countries**.

SSROC's public summary says its findings are:

1. financial incentives increase repair uptake;
2. incentives can build longer-term repair habits;
3. repair can materially reduce waste;
4. local governments can successfully lead repair programs;
5. e-waste and whitegoods are strong target categories.

Phase 2 is testing this locally.

Sources:

### Why this matters for Dubbo

Dubbo does not need to invent the policy from zero.

A practical path is:

> **ask SSROC/NSW EPA for the Phase 1 framework and copy/adapt the Phase 2 methodology for a regional-city pilot.**

# 5. Repair Cafés: useful, but only if designed well

## 5.1 Repair Cafés do work

Repair Café International's RepairMonitor recorded:

- more than **37,000 repair attempts in 2024**;
- **62% successful repairs**.

Historical data commonly shows around:
- 53–55% successful electric/electronic repairs;
- higher success for simpler mechanical/non-electrical items.

Common fixes include:
- replacement parts;
- cleaning;
- descaling;
- lubrication;
- soldering;
- simple maintenance.

The biggest recurring repair-failure reason is:

> **parts unavailable or too expensive.**

Sources:
- https://www.repaircafe.org/en/repairmonitor-factsheet-provides-insight-into-repair-data-for-2024/
- https://www.repaircafe.org/en/repairmonitor-replacing-a-part-is-most-common-procedure/

---

## 5.2 A Repair Café changes culture, not just devices

Research shows Repair Cafés can:
- make repair visible;
- share practical skills;
- make repair feel normal;
- connect people with repairers;
- improve confidence;
- demonstrate that broken objects still have value.

A 2024 repair-café study found visitors were strongly motivated by:
- environmental concern;
- access to skills/materials they did not have;
- wanting to demonstrate the importance of repair;
- belonging to the broader repair movement.

### Critical nuance

Not everybody wants to learn DIY repair.

Research also finds Repair Cafés can drift into being a free repair service rather than teaching skills.

That is not necessarily a failure if the goal is waste avoidance, but expectations need to be clear.

---

## 5.3 Hybrid repair hubs may suit Dubbo better than a once-a-month café alone

A 2026 study examined:

- a weekly appointment-based **Herstel Hub Elektro**;
- workplace/corporate Repair Cafés.

The weekly hub:
- charged a modest contribution;
- mixed paid repairers and volunteers;
- used appointments;
- repaired 234 devices over 75 sessions;
- had a 42% successful fix rate.

Corporate repair events:
- brought repair to people at work;
- assessed 109 devices;
- fixed about 41%.

This hybrid structure addresses two weaknesses of classic volunteer-only repair cafés:
- unpredictability;
- volunteer burnout/capacity.

### Dubbo model

A strong local model could be:

**Monthly community Repair Café**
+
**weekly/fortnightly booked Fix-It session**
+
**paid local repairers for difficult/safety-critical work**
+
**workplace pop-up repair days**

# 7. The 10 strongest incentives for Dubbo

## 1. A “Dubbo Fix-It Bonus”

### Proposed pilot design

Council/EPA/partners subsidise:

> **50% of eligible repair cost, capped at $75–$100 per item**

Possible starting categories:
- laptops/computers;
- phones/tablets;
- TVs;
- small appliances;
- selected whitegoods;
- bicycles.

Use approved local repairers.

### Why 50%?

It mirrors the strongest international repair-bonus models and materially moves repair cost below replacement cost.

### Why cap it?

Prevents public funding being consumed by a few expensive jobs and makes the pilot easier to evaluate.

### Eligibility rule

Only subsidise when:
- repairer believes the item can safely receive meaningful additional life;
- quote is below a defined proportion of equivalent replacement value;
- repair complies with electrical/consumer-safety law.

Do not subsidise obvious uneconomic repairs merely to hit a target.

---

## 2. Free diagnosis / “Repair Check”

One of the strongest interventions would cost much less than paying for whole repairs.

Offer:

> **Free 10–20 minute diagnosis and repair-vs-replace recommendation.**

People often replace an item without ever discovering:
- a loose cable;
- failed battery;
- blocked filter;
- broken power supply;
- cheap switch;
- software fault;
- dirty connector.

Repair Café data repeatedly shows simple maintenance is enough to restore some products.

### Behavioural objective

Make:

> **“Get it checked before replacing it.”**

the local norm.

---

## 3. Publish a trusted “Repair Dubbo” directory

One page/app should answer:

- Who repairs this?
- Where?
- What brands/categories?
- Call-out/drop-off?
- Typical diagnosis fee?
- Typical turnaround?
- Warranty on repair?
- Genuine/aftermarket parts?
- Accessibility?
- Can they repair it on site?
- Can they collect?
- If they cannot repair it, where should it go next?

### Why this matters

Research consistently identifies **search effort and service availability** as major barriers.

People should not need to make six calls to find out who fixes a microwave, laptop, TV, mower or vacuum.

---

## 4. Give repairs a warranty

A local incentive scheme should require participating commercial repairers to provide a clear warranty appropriate to the repair.

The purpose is psychological as much as legal.

People compare:

**new product + warranty**
versus
**repair + uncertainty**

A warranty narrows that trust gap.

Marketing:

> **Fixed locally. Backed by a repair warranty.**

---

## 5. Offer loan devices / temporary substitutes

For phones and laptops especially, **downtime is a major hidden cost**.

Dubbo eWaste or a partner could maintain:

- simple loan laptops;
- temporary phones;
- chargers/power supplies.

A person is much more likely to approve a 3–5 day repair if they can still work, study or communicate.

This is particularly valuable for:
- families;
- students;
- small businesses;
- people who cannot afford a second device.

---

## 6. Build a local donor-parts bank

Repair fails surprisingly often because:
- part not available;
- part too expensive;
- manufacturer no longer supplies it.

Dubbo eWaste is ideally positioned to build a controlled donor-parts inventory from equipment that is genuinely uneconomic to repair.

Examples:
- laptop screens;
- RAM;
- SSDs;
- keyboards;
- fans;
- hinges;
- chargers;
- power boards;
- TV boards;
- speakers;
- buttons/switches.

This can lower local repair price.

It also turns the business into repair infrastructure rather than only an e-waste outlet.

---

## 7. Monthly Dubbo Repair Café

Run it as a **community service separate from commercial intake**.

Possible stations:
- computers/Linux;
- small appliances;
- electrical diagnosis (within legal competency boundaries);
- bicycles;
- textiles;
- furniture/simple mechanical;
- toys.

Key design:

- bookings + walk-ins;
- one item per person initially;
- trained volunteers;
- repairer safety rules;
- visitor participates where practical;
- parts paid by owner;
- collect RepairMonitor-style data;
- clear exit route for unrepairable items.

Repair Café International supplies:
- operating manual;
- forms;
- liability templates;
- communications kit;
- RepairMonitor access.

Source:
https://www.repaircafe.org/en/join/start-your-own/

---

## 8. Workplace “Fix-It Days”

Instead of asking everyone to travel somewhere:

> bring repair to employers.

Examples:
- once per quarter;
- staff bring a broken small item;
- technician/volunteer triages it at lunch;
- item either fixed, quoted or routed correctly.

The 2026 corporate Repair Café study shows workplace events can make repair accessible.

Potential benefits:
- no extra trip for resident;
- employers can sponsor repair vouchers;
- makes repair socially visible;
- can connect Dubbo eWaste with business ITAD relationships.

---

## 9. Use social proof, not guilt

NSW EPA behaviour-change trials found visible signage, staff prompts, actual customer stories and social-media reinforcement mattered.

Its café reuse trial found:
- incentives can work;
- but uptake falls again when promotion stops;
- seeing real local people taking part is powerful.

### Dubbo campaign

Show real local outcomes:

> “Kylie's laptop: $89 repair instead of $850 replacement.”

> “Tom's vacuum: cleaned switch + $12 part. Back working.”

> “This TV needed one board, not a skip bin.”

Publish:
- before/after;
- price saved;
- kilograms avoided;
- device age;
- repair time;
- who repaired it.

Make repair visible and normal.

Do not lead with tonnes of CO2.

Lead with:

> **“Saved $600 and kept it another three years.”**

Then add the environmental benefit.

---

## 10. Make Council itself repair-first

Public messaging works better if the institution models the behaviour.

Council could adopt:

> **repair / refurbish / redeploy assessment before replacement**

for:
- laptops;
- phones;
- monitors;
- furniture;
- appliances;
- tools.

Then publish the savings.

This creates:
- procurement demand;
- local repair jobs;
- evidence;
- credibility.

# 9. What should the voucher amount be?

Do not lock in a number before talking to local repairers.

The strongest evidence suggests the key variable is the **repair-to-replacement price ratio**.

A practical Dubbo pilot should test whether the incentive can move a repair from:

> “not worth it”

to:

> “obviously worth it.”

### Candidate trial tiers

#### Small electronics
- 50% of repair;
- max $50–$75.

#### Laptops/phones/tablets/TVs
- 50%;
- max $75–$100.

#### Whitegoods
- max $100–$150;
- potentially higher where avoiding replacement clearly saves hundreds of dollars.

### Alternative

Instead of subsidising the repair itself:

> pay the diagnosis fee.

For some people, uncertainty is the bigger barrier than final cost.

Example:

**Council pays first $30–$50 diagnosis; customer decides after receiving a written quote.**

This could be substantially cheaper while still changing behaviour.

The long-term answer should **not** be Dubbo ratepayers alone.

Potential funding layers:

## Local government / NSW EPA

A pilot can establish whether it works.

NSW EPA's Local Government Waste Solutions Fund explicitly prioritises:
- reuse;
- repair;
- circular economy services;
- measurable waste reduction.

SSROC's repair-incentive pilot was funded through this route.

## Product stewardship / manufacturers

The logical longer-term model is producer-funded repair incentives.

SSROC's recent whitegoods work models an extended producer responsibility approach where a levy funds:
- better recycling;
- repair/reuse;
- repair-sector jobs.

## Retailers / brands

Retailers could offer:

> “repair credit before replacement credit.”

Instead of only:
- upgrade;
- trade-in;
- dispose.

## Employers

Employers can sponsor workplace Repair Café vouchers.

## Community / philanthropy

For low-income households:
- repair grants;
- donor parts;
- refurbished replacement if repair is impossible.

# 11. What NOT to do

## Don't rely on awareness campaigns alone

“Repair is good for the planet” will not beat:

- expensive quote;
- three-week wait;
- unavailable part;
- no local repairer.

## Don't make repair inconvenient

A voucher requiring:
- multiple forms;
- reimbursement months later;
- approved repairers 100 km away

will fail.

The discount should ideally be deducted **at point of sale**, with the repairer claiming reimbursement.

## Don't create demand before repair capacity

If a campaign doubles repair requests but nobody can take the jobs, consumers learn:

> repair is slow and difficult.

That can worsen perceptions.

## Don't romanticise DIY

Electrical appliances and lithium batteries carry real safety risks.

Community repair needs clear boundaries for:
- licensed electrical work;
- mains-powered devices;
- batteries;
- damaged devices.

## Don't turn a Repair Café into another waste drop-off

Repair Café International explicitly says visitors retain responsibility for their items and the service is not a disposal location.

## Don't shame replacement

Sometimes replacement is correct:
- safety;
- severe damage;
- parts unavailable;
- repair exceeds sensible value;
- major efficiency gains;
- unsupported device.

The scheme needs credibility by saying **no** when repair is genuinely poor value.

# 12. Repairability at point of purchase

Behaviour has to change before products break too.

Dubbo could promote:

> **“Buy something you can repair.”**

For larger purchases, encourage people to ask:

- Are parts available?
- For how many years?
- Is battery replacement possible?
- Can independent repairers work on it?
- Is there a service manual?
- What does common repair cost?
- How long is software/security support?
- Can the product be opened without destroying it?

France uses mandatory repairability/durability information for some categories.

Australian researchers in 2026 recommend repairability labelling as part of a broader Australian repair strategy.

A future Australian **Repair Star Rating** has also been proposed by NSW local-government bodies.

---

# 13. Schools and young people

Repair culture is learned.

Repair Café International offers a classroom Repair Café model.

Dubbo could partner repair volunteers/repair businesses with schools for:

- basic fault finding;
- how products work;
- sewing/mending;
- bike maintenance;
- laptop upgrades;
- safe disassembly;
- Linux on unsupported PCs;
- consumer rights / warranties;
- circular economy.

### Why this matters

If children only see:

> broken = bin / replace

that becomes normal consumer behaviour.

If they see:

> diagnose → maintain → repair → reuse → recycle last

the social norm changes.

For Dubbo:

A **Linux / old laptop clinic** could:

This is especially aligned with Dubbo eWaste and the DadLAN-style reuse skill set.

# 15. Regional equity

A good repair scheme must work for people who:

- do not drive;
- live outside central Dubbo;
- work full time;
- cannot afford an upfront repair;
- cannot go without a device;
- are not technically confident.

Therefore include:

- Saturday repair sessions;
- after-work slots;
- mobile/pop-up events in Wellington/villages later;
- point-of-sale subsidy rather than reimbursement;
- loan device pool;
- plain-English booking;
- phone booking as well as online;
- accessibility.

---

# 16. Inclusion matters

Research has identified problems in some Repair Cafés where technical volunteer culture can become male-dominated and visitors, particularly women, may be less actively encouraged to participate in the repair themselves.

A Dubbo model should deliberately:

- recruit women repairers/volunteers;
- include textiles, toys, furniture and electronics rather than only “blokes with soldering irons”;
- train volunteers in teaching/customer interaction;
- welcome people who only want the item repaired;
- create beginner-friendly stations;
- include young people.

A Repair Café should feel like a community service, not a club people need technical credibility to enter.

---

# 17. Commercial repairers must benefit

A publicly supported repair culture should not undercut the very repair businesses needed to sustain it.

Recommended split:

### Repair Café

Use for:
- diagnosis;
- simple fixes;
- maintenance;
- education;
- low-value items where commercial repair is uneconomic.

### Commercial network

Refer:
- specialist faults;
- licensed electrical work;
- board repair;
- phone screens/batteries;
- whitegoods;
- commercial IT;
- safety-critical work.

### Repair bonus

Payable through participating repair businesses.

This turns public money into **local trade revenue**, not only volunteer activity.

## Step 1 — map current repair capacity

Council already has:

- expand;
- change voucher;

# 21. Best single policy recommendation

If Dubbo could do only one thing:

> **Create a Repair First pilot where residents get a free diagnosis plus a 50% repair subsidy (capped), redeemed instantly through participating local repairers, alongside a monthly community Repair Café.**

Why this combination?

- free diagnosis gets repair considered;
- subsidy fixes the price gap;
- local repairer network solves search/trust;
- warranty reduces perceived risk;
- Repair Café builds social norm and skills;
- data identifies structural barriers;
- unrepairable items flow into reuse/parts/recycling rather than back into rubbish.

# 22. Evidence summary

| Barrier | Evidence | Dubbo response |
|---|---|---|
| repair too expensive | strongest consumer factor | repair bonus |
| repair not considered | many replace without checking | free Repair Check |

# Bottom line

If Dubbo wants residents to repair rather than replace, the answer is not:

> **“Tell people to be less wasteful.”**

It is:

> **Make repair cheaper, make it visible, make it trustworthy, remove the hassle, give people somewhere to go, and prove with local stories that repairing is the normal smart choice.**

For Dubbo eWaste, the strategic opportunity is broader than recycling:

> **become part of the infrastructure that makes repair easy before something ever becomes e-waste.**
