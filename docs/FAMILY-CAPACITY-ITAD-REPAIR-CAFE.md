# Family-capacity review — Dubbo ITAD + Community Computer Repair Café

**Research date:** 6 October 2026  
**Status:** research recommendation for pilot design; not a substitute for family agreement, Council/insurance requirements, or the operational launch gates elsewhere in this repository.  
**Privacy note:** this public repository deliberately does not record private family schedules, health information, children’s names, appointment details or residential identifiers.

---

## Executive conclusion

The two ideas are **compatible**, but they should not both become standing weekly commitments.

A small commercial **Dubbo ITAD / refurbishment pilot** and a non-commercial **community Computer Repair Café** can sit beside each other because they solve different problems:

- **ITAD:** organisational devices are formally transferred, tracked, sanitised, refurbished/reused/resold where appropriate, then recycled only when necessary.
- **Repair Café:** residents keep ownership of their devices, stay with them, learn, attempt straightforward repairs with volunteers, and take the device home or receive a referral.

The strongest operating recommendation is:

> **Do not run “ITAD every Tuesday + Repair Café every Saturday” as the default.**

Instead:

1. keep ITAD appointment-only and capped, using a small protected Tuesday block only when household/childcare coverage genuinely exists;
2. start the Repair Café **monthly**, not weekly;
3. keep at least most Saturdays as family/rest days;
4. do not let Repair Café devices become an intake funnel for the commercial ITAD venture;
5. review the arrangement after an 8–12 week pilot before increasing frequency.

This is not because community service or entrepreneurship are inherently bad for family life. The evidence suggests the problem is **sustained work–family conflict, long hours, weekend work and lack of recovery/control**. Volunteering can also improve wellbeing, purpose and social connection when it is bounded and sustainable.

# 1. What current Australian time-use data says

The latest ABS Time Use Survey is a useful reality check.

In 2024:

- parents in couple families with children under 15 who participated in unpaid work spent an average of **6 hours 16 minutes per day** on unpaid work;
- male parents who participated in unpaid work averaged **5 hours 2 minutes per day**;
- male parents who looked after children averaged **3 hours 29 minutes per day** doing child-care activities;
- parents reported substantially less free time than non-parents: **3 hours 38 minutes** versus **6 hours 5 minutes** among participants;
- **51% of people in couple families with children under 15** said they always or often felt rushed or pressed for time;
- across people who felt rushed, **balancing work and family was the most common reason (42%)**.

These are population averages, not a formula for one household. But they establish that a non-work day for a parent is **not normally an empty day available to be converted wholesale into a second job or volunteer shift**.

Source:
- ABS, *How Australians use their time, 2024*  
  https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/people-and-communities/how-australians-use-their-time/2024

# 2. Weekend work matters more than the raw hour count suggests

Australian Institute of Family Studies research using Growing Up in Australia data found that:

- longer paid-work hours were associated with less parent-child time;
- the **frequency of weekend work** was specifically associated with reduced weekend parent-child time;
- for fathers, weekend work was particularly important in explaining differences in time with children;
- parents do not necessarily recover all lost weekend family time elsewhere.

The research is older than the 2024 ABS survey and should not be treated as a precise prediction for one family. It is still useful because it addresses the exact mechanism at issue: converting weekends into regular work time.

Sources:
- AIFS, *Parental time with children*  
  https://aifs.gov.au/all-research/research-reports/parental-time-children
- AIFS, *Long hours and longings*  
  https://aifs.gov.au/all-research/research-snapshots/long-hours-and-longings

# 3. Work–family conflict is the risk, not meaningful work itself

AIFS longitudinal research found that when fathers moved into high work–family conflict, parenting capability, couple relationship quality and their own wellbeing deteriorated; moving out of high work–family conflict was associated with improvement.

A 2023 systematic review of 25 studies found the direct relationship between parental work–family conflict and children's mental health was **mixed rather than deterministic**. About half of examined associations were significant and half were not. However, parenting characteristics and parental mental health were important mediating pathways in many studies.

A newer 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of longitudinal studies found work-to-family conflict was associated with worse adult mental-health outcomes including burnout and distress. Observational evidence cannot prove that work–family conflict alone causes these outcomes, but the direction across the literature is consistent enough to justify conservative scheduling.

The practical lesson is not:

> “Never run a side business while parenting.”

It is:

> **Design the side business so that it does not routinely make family time rushed, fragmented or secondary.**

# 4. Business guidance also argues for hard boundaries

Australian Government business guidance explicitly warns that:

- a new business may require long hours and weekends, reducing time for family, friends and hobbies;
- a side hustle can create burnout if it expands into extended hours;
- home-based business owners should set work hours, take breaks and create physical and temporal boundaries between home and work;
- new business owners often carry sales, finance, marketing, administration and operations at the same time, so the hidden administrative load matters as well as the visible customer hours.

That hidden load is important here. A three-hour intake session is not only three hours. It can also generate:

- messages and bookings;
- sourcing parts;
- testing and follow-up;
- listings;
- record keeping;
- data-sanitisation administration;
- Council/insurance/compliance work;
- volunteer coordination;
- event setup/pack-down;
- customer questions after the event.

# 5. Repair Cafés do not need to run every week

The 2026 Repair Café International global survey reported that Repair Cafés are **usually held once a month**, although frequency varies widely.

That is a major design clue for Dubbo.

Repair Café International describes the model as voluntary and non-commercial. Its starter material is designed around a local group, a venue, repair volunteers, registration/liability forms and shared repair rather than one person operating a standing repair shop.

Current NSW precedent also supports short, bounded events. Waverley Council's 2026 Repair Café sessions have generally been around **2½ hours**, and its computer/phone service uses **30-minute repair slots**. The owner brings the device, charger and passwords/PINs if needed, backs up important data beforehand, and supplies upgrade parts such as RAM/SSD.

A Dubbo pilot can therefore be useful without becoming a weekly Saturday occupation.

# 6. Volunteering can be positive — if it remains volunteering rather than another obligation

Volunteering Australia reports that volunteering is associated with greater life satisfaction, happiness, self-esteem, social connection, sense of purpose and community wellbeing.

This matters because the Repair Café should not be treated only as a cost to family life. A bounded community project can be meaningful and energising.

The problem arises when:

- the organiser becomes indispensable;
- sessions are too frequent;
- admin expands into evenings;
- family repeatedly has to work around the project;
- commercial and volunteer work blur together;
- saying “not this week” becomes difficult because customers or volunteers expect availability.

Sources:
- Volunteering Australia, *Volunteering and wellbeing* (April 2026)

# 7. Public-safe operator capacity assessment

A private schedule review was completed on 6 October 2026. Personal appointment details are intentionally excluded from this public repository.

The relevant non-sensitive conclusions are:

- the operator already has a four-day main-job week;
- Tuesday is the principal non-main-job weekday but is **not consistently an empty day**;
- Saturdays have high family/recovery value and should not be presumed to be spare business capacity;
- Sunday already contains recurring faith/family commitments;
- turning both Tuesday and Saturday into standing project days would create structured obligations across nearly the whole week.

Therefore the limiting factor is not technical enthusiasm or whether there are enough hours on paper.

It is **recovery, parenting availability, household flexibility and cognitive spillover**.

# 8. Capacity scenarios

These are planning scenarios, not claims about exact future hours.

## Scenario A — both projects every week

Assume:

- ITAD: 3 hours each Tuesday;
- Repair Café: 3 hours each Saturday;
- combined booking/admin/follow-up: 2 hours per week.

That is about:

- **8 extra hours/week**
- **416 hours/year**
- equivalent to about **10.4 forty-hour working weeks** of extra annual commitment.

This also creates a six- or seven-day rhythm of scheduled obligations once the main job, Sunday commitments and project administration are considered.

**Assessment:** not recommended as the starting model.

## Scenario B — weekly ITAD + monthly Repair Café

Assume:

- one 3-hour ITAD block each week;
- one 3-hour Repair Café plus about 1 hour event administration each month.

That is about:

- **204 hours/year**
- **3.9 hours/week averaged across the year**.

This may be feasible if the ITAD block occurs only when childcare/household coverage is real and if it does not migrate into evenings.

**Assessment:** possible, but still requires an 8–12 week family-capacity trial.

## Scenario C — fortnightly ITAD + monthly Repair Café

Assume:

- one 3-hour ITAD block every two weeks;
- one 3-hour Repair Café plus about 1 hour event administration each month.

That is about:

- **126 hours/year**
- **2.4 hours/week averaged across the year**.

**Assessment:** best starting capacity model.

It creates enough real activity to test both ideas without requiring the household to behave as though two new weekly organisations already exist.

# 9. Recommended 8–12 week dual-pilot

## Dubbo ITAD

Start with:

- **one 2–3 hour appointment/processing block per fortnight**;
- Tuesday preferred only where family/childcare coverage genuinely exists;
- pre-approved devices only;
- no all-day opening;
- no routine evening catch-up;
- no Saturday intake on a Repair Café weekend;
- maximum first pilot remains 20–30 devices.

The business can still receive enquiries at any time, but **the operator should not promise immediate physical availability**.

## Computer Repair Café

Start with:

- **one Saturday per month**;
- approximately 2½–3 hours;
- community venue rather than the home shed if Council/partner support permits;
- 30–45 minute device slots;
- one device per visitor;
- straightforward laptop/desktop diagnosis and repair only;
- no overnight custody;
- owner stays with the device;
- complex jobs referred onward;
- several volunteers so the event does not depend on one person.

This follows the normal Repair Café cadence much more closely than weekly Saturday sessions.

# 10. Family-protection rules

These are project governance controls, not just lifestyle aspirations.

1. **Family time is a launch gate.** Do not scale because demand exists if the household does not have capacity.
2. **No standing weekly Saturday business commitment during Phase 0.**
3. **Protect at least three Saturdays in a normal month from Repair Café obligations.**
4. **Do not routinely schedule project work through family dinner/bedtime routines.**
5. **Do not work on customer devices while simultaneously responsible for direct child supervision.**
6. **No damaged/swollen lithium-battery work in a family environment.**
7. **No commercial ITAD intake during the Repair Café event.**
8. **No automatic transfer of unwanted Repair Café devices into commercial stock.**
9. **No expectation that a partner/family member becomes unpaid project labour.**
10. **Keep a simple weekly hour log, including admin and messages, not only bench time.**
11. **A missed project session is preferable to making the household reorganise around routine customer expectations.**
12. **The pilot must remain easy to pause.**

---

# 11. Stop / reduce triggers

Reduce the frequency or pause one project if any of the following becomes a repeated pattern:

- project work spills into several evenings per week;
- Saturdays stop functioning primarily as family/rest time;
- family routines are regularly missed because of bookings or repairs;
- the operator is frequently rushed, irritable, mentally absent or preoccupied during family time;
- the household repeatedly has to absorb unexpected customer/project commitments;
- sleep/recovery is being traded for project administration;
- customer demand starts determining family availability;
- unfinished stock accumulates and creates pressure to “catch up” on weekends;
- the Repair Café cannot run without the operator personally carrying most roles;
- either project is being kept alive mainly because of sunk effort rather than demonstrated value.

These triggers deliberately focus on **spillover**, because that is where the strongest work–family evidence points.

---

# 12. Keep the two ideas structurally separate

The strongest combined ecosystem is:

**Residents → Repair Café → repair/learn → professional repair referral if needed → lawful recycling if truly end-of-life**

and separately:

**businesses/schools/organisations → ITAD → chain of custody → sanitisation → test/grade → reuse/refurbish/resell/social reuse → downstream recycling**

The projects can share:

- general repair knowledge;
- safety standards;
- volunteer relationships;
- repair guides;
- community education;
- knowledge of local professional repairers;
- understanding of downstream e-waste routes.

They should not automatically share:

- customer devices;
- inventory;
- money;
- Council/community funding;
- client lists;
- commercial leads;
- ownership transfer;
- parts without a documented donation/transfer;
- private data.

This separation protects community trust and reduces the risk that a Council-supported Repair Café looks like a lead-generation or stock-sourcing channel for a private business.

---

# 13. Pilot measures

For the first 8–12 weeks, record both **project outcomes** and **family-capacity outcomes**.

## Project

- ITAD devices accepted;
- devices successfully reused;
- average labour per device;
- revenue/contribution where applicable;
- Repair Café visitors;
- repair success/referral/recycling outcomes;
- volunteer numbers;
- event admin hours.

## Capacity

- total ITAD hours;
- total Repair Café hours;
- admin/message hours;
- evening spillover occurrences;
- family routines displaced;
- Saturdays consumed;
- number of times work had to be postponed for family;
- subjective weekly rating: calm / manageable / stretched / unsustainable.

The capacity data is as important as device volume.

---

# 14. Decision after the pilot

Only increase frequency if all of the following are true:

- household capacity remains genuinely comfortable;
- project administration stays inside defined hours;
- most Saturdays remain protected;
- there is demonstrated demand;
- the ITAD economics justify the operator time;
- the Repair Café has enough volunteers that it is not a one-person service;
- Council/insurance/venue requirements are satisfied;
- the projects remain cleanly separated.

Possible next steps after successful pilot:

- ITAD could move from fortnightly to weekly Tuesday blocks;
- Repair Café could remain monthly, which is already normal internationally;
- a second monthly Repair Café should only be considered if volunteer leadership is shared rather than simply doubling the organiser’s workload.

---

# 15. Bottom line

The two ideas **can strengthen each other without consuming the family**.

The mistake would be treating every nominally “free” Tuesday and Saturday as unused capacity.

The evidence supports a more conservative sequence:

> **small ITAD block → monthly Repair Café → measure actual workload and family spillover → scale only what the household can carry calmly**

For Phase 0, the recommended capacity ceiling is:

> **fortnightly ITAD + monthly Repair Café for 8–12 weeks.**

That is enough activity to learn whether both ideas deserve to grow, while keeping family availability and recovery as explicit operating constraints rather than whatever time is left over after the projects expand.
