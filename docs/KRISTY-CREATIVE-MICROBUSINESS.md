# Kristy creative microbusiness pathway

Updated: 2 October 2026

This note asks a simple question: **how could Kristy follow the same kind of progression as Elise Parker / The Painted Brush & Co, without needing to copy furniture restoration itself?**

The useful part of Elise's story is the sequence:

**interest/hobby -> Facebook page -> a few dollars -> home-based work -> repeat customers -> larger home workspace -> physical retail/workshop space -> warehouse only after demand exists**

For Kristy, the strongest low-risk version to test first is **visual art / painting and small handmade creative products**, with **creative workshops as a later-stage option** if she enjoys selling and teaching.

This document deliberately avoids health or other private personal information. It focuses only on the business pathway and public market evidence.

---

## Executive recommendation

Do **not** start by registering an elaborate brand, renting premises, building a website, buying a lot of stock or trying to become a full creative studio.

Start the way Elise did in 2015:

1. make things;
2. put them on a simple social page;
3. sell a few;
4. learn what people actually want;
5. reinvest only money the activity earns;
6. move to markets/workshops only when demand is visible.

### Best first model

**Small original paintings / illustrations + a Facebook or Instagram page + local pickup/postage.**

Why this is the best first test:

- very little storage compared with furniture;
- no need for customers to visit the house;
- easy to photograph and post online;
- individual pieces can be made one at a time;
- no need to commit to a fixed product range;
- Dubbo has strong evidence of an active art-buying community;
- the same original art can later become prints, cards or workshop examples.

# Product paths worth testing

## Path 1 — original small artworks

**Best first test.**

Examples:

- small paintings;
- drawings;
- simple framed works;
- local-landscape / botanical / family-friendly pieces;
- small gift-sized art rather than large expensive canvases.

Advantages:

- low stock complexity;
- each item is unique;
- simple local Facebook/Instagram selling;
- suitable for Rotunda Market and eventually Dubbo Art Fair;
- unsold work is not perishable stock.

Risk:

- originals can take too long relative to sale price.

Measure **hours per piece** from the start.

---

## Path 2 — prints, cards and small reproductions

Once 2-3 original works get genuine interest, turn the strongest designs into lower-priced products.

Possible products:

- A5/A4 prints;
- greeting cards;
- bookmarks;
- small framed prints;
- limited runs rather than large inventory.

This gives people who like the art but cannot justify an original another way to buy.

Do not print 100 units first. Start with tiny runs or print-on-demand/local short-run printing.

---

## Path 3 — commissions

Only add commissions after people independently ask for them.

Potential examples:

- family/home illustrations;
- meaningful places;
- pet portraits;
- personalised children's-room art.

Commissions can pay better than speculative inventory, but they also introduce deadlines and customer expectations. Keep the first few tightly scoped.

---

## Path 4 — creative workshops

This is the most interesting **later-stage** opportunity.

Dubbo already supports paid guided creative experiences:

- a November 2026 guided paint-and-sip event is advertised at **$59 per person**;
- September 2026 ceramic-painting classes in Dubbo were advertised at **$99-$109**.

Those are not price recommendations for Kristy. They simply demonstrate that local people already pay for guided creative experiences.

Sources:
- https://www.nsw.gov.au/visiting-and-exploring-nsw/nsw-events/80s-and-90s-singalong-paint-and-sip-dubbo
- https://www.nsw.gov.au/visiting-and-exploring-nsw/nsw-events/ceramic-painting-class-dubbo

### Sensible sequence

Do not begin with workshops.

First establish that Kristy:

1. enjoys making consistently;
2. can finish work to a standard she is happy selling;
3. enjoys interacting with buyers;
4. enjoys showing another person how she makes something.

Then test a **very small hosted workshop** at an existing venue rather than inviting groups into the rental home.

---

## Path 5 — furniture/upcycling later, not first

Elise's exact product was furniture, but copying that part creates unnecessary friction for Kristy at this stage:

- bulky stock;
- transport;
- sanding/dust/noise;
- paint/finish storage;
- large workspace;
- much more conflict with residential/rental use;
- harder unsold-inventory problem.

If Kristy later discovers she loves furniture restoration, it can become another stream. It should not be assumed merely because that was Elise's route.

# Selling channels: cheapest first

## Stage 1 — social page

The closest analogue to Elise's original Facebook page is still a simple social presence.

Start with:

- Facebook page or Instagram account;
- clean photos in natural light;
- item dimensions;
- price displayed clearly;
- local pickup by arrangement or postage;
- no expensive logo/site/branding package.

The goal is **evidence of purchase**, not follower count.

## Stage 2 — local market

Use the Rotunda Market as the first face-to-face trial once there is enough finished stock.

The test is not just revenue. Record:

- what people stop at;
- what they touch/pick up;
- common questions;
- which prices get immediate interest;
- which work receives compliments but no purchase;
- whether market selling feels energising or draining.

## Stage 3 — online marketplace

Etsy becomes useful if Kristy develops work that can ship repeatedly, particularly prints/cards or repeatable handmade products.

Current Etsy policy charges a **6.5% transaction fee**. For Australian domestic Etsy Payments, processing is currently **3% + A$0.25 per order**, with other listing/setup/advertising fees potentially applying.

Sources:
- https://www.etsy.com/au/legal/fees
- https://www.etsy.com/legal/etsy-payments/

Because of those fees, Etsy is not necessary to validate the idea. Local direct selling is cheaper at the beginning.

# Hobby first, business when it becomes a business

The ATO does not define this simply by whether someone earns their first $20 or $100.

Relevant indicators include whether the activity:

- has a commercial purpose;
- has a purpose and prospect of profit;
- is repeated/regular/continuous;
- is organised in a business-like way;
- is better characterised as a business than a hobby.

Source:
- https://www.ato.gov.au/api/public/content/9f4dee57-0722-4802-b1aa-d9a94d6b0330_8f1d0b40_14e0_426c_b95d_4ac04a0e7a36_pdf

### Practical rule for the pilot

It is fine for the first step to be genuinely exploratory.

But once Kristy deliberately starts producing regularly **for profit**, advertising, taking repeat orders or operating systematically, treat it as a business and sort out the appropriate ABN/name/tax records rather than continuing to label it a hobby.

Keep a simple spreadsheet from sale #1 anyway:

- date;
- item;
- materials cost;
- hours;
- sale price;
- selling/platform fee;
- postage;
- actual profit.

That record will be useful regardless of when the tax classification changes.

# Home / rental position

NSW Planning says low-impact home enterprises can operate from the dwelling or an attached/detached garage or studio if they meet the relevant standards. It specifically includes home businesses and home industries, and small online retail can fit within the framework.

Business.gov.au also lists handmade goods/crafts as a common form of home business, while warning renters to check their lease/landlord and insurance.

Sources:
- https://www.planningportal.nsw.gov.au/development-and-assessment/planning-approval-pathways/exempt-development/home-based-enterprises
- https://business.gov.au/Planning/Business-structures-and-types/Home-based-businesses

### Specific implication for the current rental

Earlier review of the tenancy agreement found **no blanket ban on business activity**, but it does contain a specific garage/car-space term restricting that area to vehicle parking rather than storage of personal goods.

A tiny art business is therefore substantially easier to fit around the tenancy than a bulky furniture business or public workshop venue:

- make/store a small amount indoors or in an otherwise permitted area;
- sell online;
- use markets/external venues for customer-facing activity;
- avoid regular customer traffic at the house;
- get written clarification before the activity materially changes how the property is used.

# A 90-day "Painted Brush style" pilot for Kristy

## Weeks 1-2 — make, don't brand

Goal: create a tiny body of work.

- Choose **one medium** to start.
- Finish 6 pieces.
- Record material cost and time.
- Photograph all six consistently.
- Do not buy bulk packaging, signage or a website.

## Weeks 3-4 — Facebook-page stage

Goal: find out whether anyone will actually pay.

- Create a simple social page.
- Post the 6 finished pieces with real prices.
- Offer local pickup/postage.
- Tell friends/community contacts once, without turning it into a giant launch.
- Target: first 3 genuine paid sales, not likes.

If nobody buys, change the product/price/presentation before making 30 more pieces.

## Month 2 — build only from evidence

Goal: identify the strongest product.

- Make more of the style/size that got actual purchasing interest.
- Test one lower-priced reproduction/product if an original attracts interest.
- Accept at most 1-2 tightly scoped commissions if customers request them.
- Contact the Rotunda Market organiser and confirm stall requirements, fee and insurance.

Possible market dates after this research date include **25 October, 8 November, 22 November and 13 December 2026**.

## Month 3 — one public test

Goal: decide whether this deserves to become a real side business.

Run one market stall or another small public sales test.

Afterwards review:

- total revenue;
- true materials cost;
- hours worked;
- profit per hour;
- what actually sold;
- whether Kristy wants to keep doing it.

The last measure matters. A profitable creative side business that kills the enjoyment of creating is not automatically a good outcome.

---

# Suggested go / change / stop decision

After 90 days:

### Continue and slowly scale if

- strangers, not only friends/family, have bought work;
- at least one product/style has repeated demand;
- pricing covers materials and gives a worthwhile return for time;
- Kristy still wants to make the work;
- inventory is not taking over the house.

### Change direction if

- people love the work but will not pay the price needed;
- only one-off commissions sell;
- originals get interest but lower-priced prints/cards sell better;
- workshops sound more appealing than product production.

### Stop/pause without calling it failure if

- the sales process removes the joy;
- the hourly return is consistently poor;
- it adds too much clutter/admin;
- Kristy simply discovers she would rather keep art as art.

That is exactly what a cheap pilot is for.

# What to copy from Elise, and what not to copy

| Copy | Don't copy automatically |
|---|---|
| start from genuine interest | furniture as the product |
| social page before shop | buying lots of inventory |
| make a few dollars first | large formal launch |
| use existing space | customer traffic at home |
| reinvest slowly | expensive branding/site |
| expand only when cramped by success | warehouse before demand |
| adapt the model as life changes | assuming every hobby must become full-time work |

# The family-business insight

Elise's history and Joe's history show the same deeper pattern:

**build capability before overhead.**

Elise went:

**hobby -> Facebook -> rental hallway -> home shed -> shop -> warehouse.**

Joe went:

**tech experience -> own e-waste -> friends/family -> Bendigo E-Waste -> formalisation -> warehouse/commercial expansion.**

A sensible Dubbo version could therefore be two very small experiments running independently:

- **Dubbo E-Waste:** controlled electronics reuse/refurb pilot;
- **Kristy's creative work:** art/creative products, local online sales and one market test.

Neither needs to carry the financial burden of becoming a "proper business" before customers prove that it should.

# Bottom line

Kristy's strongest version of the Painted Brush story is not:

**"start a furniture business."**

It is:

**create because she genuinely wants to -> put a few pieces in front of real buyers -> make the first dollars -> discover what sells and what she enjoys -> use markets to test demand -> add repeatable products -> add workshops only if that part appeals -> move into dedicated space only when the existing setup is genuinely too small.**

That preserves the part of Elise's story that actually mattered: **the business grew behind the demand instead of ahead of it.**
